Blood Pressure Target for Diabetes: Is Under 120 Better Than Under 140?

You sit down at your checkup, the cuff tightens around your arm, and the screen shows 132. A few years ago that would have earned a nod. Now your doctor mentions aiming for under 120, which could mean another pill and a few more trips to the pharmacy.

If you live with type 2 diabetes, you've probably heard different blood pressure numbers from different places. One guideline says under 130, a newer one encourages under 120 for some people, and a friend on the same medicines was told 140 is fine. This article is for adults with type 2 diabetes who take blood pressure medicine, for the people who help them manage it, and for anyone whose doctor has brought up a lower target.

A large trial of nearly 9,500 adults with type 2 diabetes, published in November 2026, tested this question directly. People who aimed for under 120 had slightly fewer heart attacks and strokes than people who aimed for under 140, but the difference was too small to be sure it was real. Kidney problems were also more common in the lower-target group, which is why your own number is worth a conversation with your doctor rather than a change on your own.

Key Takeaways

  • In a 2026 trial of 9,476 adults with type 2 diabetes, aiming for a top number under 120 led to slightly fewer heart events than aiming for under 140, 6.0% compared with 6.7% over 4 years. The difference was too small to rule out chance.
  • Deaths were the same in both groups, at 7.8%.
  • Drops in kidney function were more common with the lower target, though kidney failure and dialysis were not.
  • Pooled with two earlier trials, aiming for under 120 lowered heart events by about 16%.
  • Current guidelines recommend under 130/80 for most people with diabetes, and under 120 when it's safe and well tolerated. Your own goal is a decision to make with your doctor.

Blood Pressure Targets for Diabetes: Why So Many People Are Confused

High blood pressure and type 2 diabetes often show up together, and each one makes the other harder on your body. Raised blood sugar damages the lining of your blood vessels over time, and high pressure pushes harder against that same lining. Diabetes raises the risk of heart disease two to three times at every level of systolic blood pressure, according to the ACCORD trial researchers. That's why your doctor watches your blood pressure almost as closely as your A1C.

Systolic pressure is the top number in your reading and shows the pressure in your arteries each time your heart beats. The bottom number, diastolic pressure, is the pressure between beats, and a reading of 130/80 or higher counts as high blood pressure in adults. For people with diabetes, the debate has mostly been about the top number and how low it should go.

The advice has moved a lot over the years. In 2010, the ACCORD researchers noted that no randomized trial had yet shown a benefit from lowering the top number below about 135 to 140 in people with type 2 diabetes. Today, the American Diabetes Association's 2026 Standards of Care recommend under 130/80 if it can be reached safely, and they encourage under 120 for people at high risk of heart or kidney disease. The 2025 AHA/ACC guideline takes a similar line, aiming for under 130/80 for most adults and encouraging a top number under 120 for people who tolerate it well.

So if you've been told 140, then 130, and now 120, you aren't imagining the shift. A lower number generally means more pills, which can also mean more side effects, more blood tests and more lightheaded moments when you stand up too fast.

The new trial included only adults with type 2 diabetes, which generally develops in adulthood when the body stops responding well to insulin, unlike type 1, where the body makes little or no insulin at all. Knowing how type 1 and type 2 diabetes differ helps you tell if these results apply to you. The trial set out to answer one practical question for this group, which is if aiming for under 120 protects the heart better than aiming for under 140.

What the OPTIMAL-DIABETES Trial Tested on Blood Pressure Targets

The OPTIMAL-DIABETES trial took place at 33 hospitals and clinics across Brazil, and it compared two blood pressure goals head to head, with results published in the New England Journal of Medicine on November 7, 2026. Between 2019 and 2021, researchers enrolled 9,476 adults with type 2 diabetes. They split them by chance into two groups, 4,745 people aiming for a top number under 120 and 4,731 aiming for under 140.

Everyone in the trial was 50 or older, and the average age was 65. Women made up 62% of the group, which is more than many heart trials manage. The mix of people was also broad, with 42.5% describing themselves as mixed race, 41.9% as white and 14.5% as Black, so the results aren't based on just one population.

To join, people needed a top number between 130 and 180 and a higher-than-average risk of heart problems, as set out in the trial's public registration. That could mean heart disease or kidney disease they already had, or other risk factors such as smoking, high cholesterol or being 75 or older. Before the trial, the average reading was 146/80, about 28.5% of people already had heart disease, and about 23% had reduced kidney function.

Doctors adjusted each person's medicines over time to reach their group's goal. Both patients and doctors knew which target they were working toward, an approach called open-label, because you can't hide a blood pressure number from the person reading the cuff. Over the trial, the under-120 group averaged a top number of about 122, while the under-140 group averaged about 135, a difference of roughly 14 points. Getting there took an average of 3.2 blood pressure medicines in the lower group, compared with 2.3 in the standard group, and about 7 in 10 people in the lower group had reached their target by month 28.

The researchers then followed everyone for about 4 years. They counted major heart events, meaning death from heart disease, a heart attack or stroke that wasn't fatal, or a hospital stay for heart failure or unstable angina, which is chest pain that comes on suddenly or at rest.

In Summary

This was a large, well-run trial in nearly 9,500 adults with type 2 diabetes who were 50 or older and had a higher chance of heart problems. One group aimed for under 120 and the other for under 140, and they ended up about 14 points apart. If you're 50 or older with type 2 diabetes and other heart risks, these are people a lot like you.

What the Trial Found About Heart Attacks and Strokes in Type 2 Diabetes

Over about 4 years, 6.0% of people in the under-120 group had a major heart event, compared with 6.7% in the under-140 group. In everyday numbers, that's about 6 fewer heart attacks, strokes or heart-related hospital stays for every 1,000 people who aimed lower. On paper, that works out to about a 10% lower risk.

But the difference was too small for the researchers to rule out chance, so the trial counts as neutral. Statisticians express this with a range of likely effects. Here the range ran from about 23% lower risk to 6% higher risk, and because it crosses zero, nobody can say for sure that the lower target helped.

Looking at heart attacks and strokes on their own, the pattern was the same. Strokes affected 1.1% of the lower-target group and 1.5% of the standard group, and heart attacks 1.2% and 1.6%, but neither difference was clear enough to rule out chance. Deaths from any cause were identical, at 7.8% in both groups.

Otavio Berwanger, one of the lead researchers, put it simply when presenting the results at the American Heart Association meeting in Chicago. He said the team "did not find a difference comparing the two" targets. Lead author Karla Santo was more hopeful, and said aiming lower "may still have modest yet meaningful health benefits" even without a clear result.

Both views fit the numbers. The trial was designed to detect a 20% drop in heart events, but the drop it found was closer to 10%, which is too small for a trial this size to prove. So the trial can't confirm a small benefit, and it can't rule one out either.

In Summary

Aiming for under 120 led to slightly fewer heart attacks and strokes than aiming for under 140, but the difference was too small to be sure it was real. The lower target may help a little, or it may not help at all. If you're already near 130 to 140 and feeling well, this trial alone doesn't show you'd be clearly better off going lower.

Lower Blood Pressure and Kidney Function in Diabetes

The trial found one clear difference between the groups, and it was in the kidneys. Kidney problems affected 5.7% of people in the under-120 group, compared with 3.7% in the under-140 group, about 55% higher. In everyday numbers, that's about 20 more people with kidney problems for every 1,000 who aimed lower.

The researchers counted it as a kidney problem if someone's kidney function fell by half or more, or if they developed kidney failure, started dialysis or needed a transplant. Almost all of the difference came from drops in kidney function, which doctors track with a blood test called eGFR, seen in 5.4% of the lower-target group and 3.4% of the standard group. Kidney failure and long-term dialysis were uncommon and happened at similar rates in both groups.

Doctors have seen this pattern before, and the researchers point out that ACCORD BP found the same thing, with more drops in kidney function but no more kidney failure. When blood pressure comes down a lot, less blood flows through the kidneys' filters, and eGFR often dips as a result. It isn't yet known if these drops lead to lasting harm, and that will take longer follow-up to answer.

Blood tests also picked up more high potassium in the lower-target group, 12.4% compared with 9.3%, and more low sodium, 4.5% compared with 3.1%. The researchers think this probably reflects the extra medicines. Falls that caused injury and fainting were rare and about the same in both groups, at under 1 in 100.

Serious adverse events overall, such as hospital stays for any reason, happened at similar rates in the two groups, 14.9% with the lower target and 14.6% with the standard one. So the extra pills didn't lead to more serious problems overall, but the kidney and potassium changes are worth keeping an eye on.

In Summary

Aiming for under 120 came with more drops in kidney function and more high potassium, though not more kidney failure, and overall serious side effects were about the same in both groups. Nobody yet knows if the kidney drops cause lasting harm. If you're on a lower target, regular kidney blood and urine tests help your doctor spot changes early.

How OPTIMAL-DIABETES Compares With ACCORD BP and BPROAD

OPTIMAL-DIABETES is the third large trial to test under 120 against under 140 in type 2 diabetes, and the three haven't agreed. That disagreement is why your doctor's advice may differ from what you read in the news.

The first was ACCORD BP in North America, published in 2010, with 4,733 adults. After a year, the lower-target group averaged 119 and the standard group 134. Aiming lower didn't significantly cut major heart events, though it did lower strokes, and serious side effects such as very low blood pressure and rising creatinine happened in 3.3% of the lower group compared with 1.3% of the standard group.

The second was BPROAD in China, published in 2024, and it's the largest of the three, with 12,821 adults followed for about 5 years. BPROAD found that aiming for under 120 cut major heart events by 21%, a result clear enough to rule out chance. It also found more episodes of low blood pressure with symptoms, such as dizziness, and more high potassium in the lower-target group.

Before the results came out, the OPTIMAL-DIABETES team had planned to pool all three trials together. Combined, aiming for under 120 lowered the risk of major heart events by 16%, according to results shared at the American Heart Association meeting, and the three trials didn't clash once pooled. BPROAD carried the most weight in that combined result, at about 45%, because it was the biggest.

The researchers think the difference with BPROAD may come down to the kind of heart events each trial counted. In China, strokes make up a much bigger share of heart problems, and strokes made up 72% of the events in BPROAD, while heart attacks are more common in Western countries and in Brazil. Differences in background, health care and treatment habits may also play a part.

That's why Berwanger described the trial as neutral while adding that the full body of evidence calls for a more careful reading. It also explains why results from people without diabetes don't carry straight over. SPRINT, an earlier US trial that found clear heart benefits from aiming for under 120, left out people with diabetes altogether.

In Summary

On its own, each trial gives a mixed answer, but pooled together, the three suggest aiming for under 120 lowers heart events by about 16% in people with type 2 diabetes. Each trial also found a different downside to aiming lower, from dizziness to kidney changes. A lower target may help your heart, but it comes with things your doctor will want to watch.

What Blood Pressure Goal Guidelines Recommend for Diabetes Now

Today's guidelines already lean toward lower numbers for people with diabetes, and they were written before this trial came out. The American Diabetes Association's 2026 Standards of Care say the goal for most people with diabetes is under 130/80 if it can be reached safely. For people at high risk of heart or kidney disease, the ADA encourages a top number under 120.

The ADA also leaves room for a looser goal when it makes sense. For older adults in good health, it recommends under 130/80 when that can be reached safely. For people in poor health, with a shorter life expectancy or with a high chance of side effects from blood pressure medicines, it suggests under 140/90 instead.

Heart doctors take a similar view. The 2025 AHA/ACC high blood pressure guideline recommends under 130/80 for most adults and encourages a top number under 120 for people who tolerate it well.

OPTIMAL-DIABETES doesn't overturn this advice, but it adds to the case for choosing a goal person by person. It only included adults 50 and older with higher heart risk, so its results don't tell us much about younger people or people at lower risk. The ADA updates its Standards every year, and the next edition will be the first one written after this trial.

Your blood pressure goal also affects more than your heart. Keeping blood pressure in range is one of the habits linked to a lower chance of dementia, which is part of why the link between type 2 diabetes and Alzheimer's risk has drawn so much attention.

Talking to Your Doctor About Your Blood Pressure Target

Please don't change or stop any blood pressure medicine because of this trial. Stopping suddenly can make your blood pressure spike, and your goal depends on things only your doctor can weigh, such as your kidney function, your other conditions and how you feel on your current pills.

Your next appointment is a good time to ask what your goal is and why. These questions can help you start the conversation.

  1. What blood pressure goal is right for me, and is it under 120, under 130 or something else?
  2. Am I at high risk of heart or kidney disease, and does that change my goal?
  3. If we aim lower, what blood tests will we do to check my kidneys and potassium, and how often?
  4. Which side effects should I tell you about straight away?

Home readings give your doctor a much clearer picture than a single reading at the clinic, where nerves can push the number up. The 2025 AHA/ACC guideline recommends home blood pressure monitoring to help confirm high readings and tailor treatment. Write down a few readings at the same time of day for a week before your appointment and bring the list with you.

Kidney checks are worth asking about too, especially if you're aiming for a lower number. Two simple tests show how your kidneys are doing, eGFR, a blood test that measures how well they filter, and the urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, which looks for protein leaking into your urine. The same guideline now recommends the urine test for everyone with high blood pressure.

Tell your doctor if you feel dizzy or lightheaded when you stand up, feel unusually tired, or notice your heart racing or slowing down. These can be signs your pressure has dropped too far or that your potassium is off, and both are easy to check.

Medicine is only part of the picture. Cutting back on salt, moving more and losing even a little weight can bring your top number down by several points, and these natural ways to lower blood pressure work alongside your pills, which may mean you need fewer of them to reach your goal.

What the OPTIMAL-DIABETES Trial Means for Your Blood Pressure Target

If you have type 2 diabetes, this trial doesn't mean your blood pressure target is wrong, and it doesn't mean you need to chase a lower number either. Aiming for under 120 gave a small heart benefit that may or may not be real, and it came with more drops in kidney function, while deaths and serious side effects stayed about the same.

Taken together with BPROAD and ACCORD BP, the evidence leans toward a modest benefit from aiming lower, as long as your kidneys and potassium are checked along the way. For most people, the sensible choice is a goal set with your doctor, home readings to track it and regular kidney tests, especially if your target is under 120.

Frequently Asked Questions About Blood Pressure Targets in Diabetes

What Is the Best Blood Pressure Target for Type 2 Diabetes?

For most adults with diabetes, the ADA recommends under 130/80 if it can be reached safely, and it encourages a top number under 120 for people at high risk of heart or kidney disease. Your doctor will set your goal based on your health, your kidneys and how well you handle your medicines.

Is a Blood Pressure of 120 Too Low for Someone With Diabetes?

No. For many people with diabetes, a top number around 120 is safe and may protect the heart. In the OPTIMAL-DIABETES trial, falls and fainting were no more common with the lower target, though drops in kidney function and high potassium were. Lower readings with dizziness or fainting are worth reporting to your doctor.

What Did the OPTIMAL-DIABETES Trial Find?

Aiming for under 120 didn't clearly reduce heart attacks, strokes or heart-related hospital stays compared with aiming for under 140 in adults with type 2 diabetes. It did come with more drops in kidney function, while serious side effects overall were about the same.

Should I Lower My Blood Pressure Medicine After This Study?

No, keep taking your medicine as prescribed. The trial didn't test cutting back on medicine, and stopping suddenly can make your blood pressure spike. Ask your doctor about your goal at your next visit instead.

Does Lowering Blood Pressure Damage the Kidneys?

Lowering blood pressure a lot can reduce kidney function on blood tests, and that happened more often with the under-120 target in this trial. Kidney failure and dialysis weren't more common, and it's not yet known if these drops cause lasting harm, so regular kidney tests are a sensible precaution.

Why Do Blood Pressure Trials in Diabetes Disagree?

Each trial included different people in different countries. BPROAD in China found a clear benefit, possibly because strokes, which lower blood pressure prevents well, made up most of its heart events. Trials in the Americas, where heart attacks are more common, found smaller benefits.
