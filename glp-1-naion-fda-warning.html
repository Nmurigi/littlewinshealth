The FDA's GLP-1 NAION Warning: What the New Eye Risk Label Means for Ozempic, Mounjaro and Similar Medicines

On 9 November 2026, the FDA told the makers of every GLP-1 medicine to add a new warning to their labels. The warning is about NAION, a rare condition that damages the optic nerve and can cause sudden vision loss in one eye.

If you take Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro, Zepbound, Trulicity or another GLP-1, this GLP-1 NAION warning applies to your medicine.

The risk is small, and the FDA hasn't proven that these drugs cause the condition. If you notice a sudden change in the vision of one eye, contact a health care professional right away. This article explains what the FDA found, how rare NAION is, which symptoms to look for and what to ask your doctor.

What the FDA's GLP-1 NAION Warning Says and Why It Was Issued

The FDA put out the warning in a Drug Safety Communication. It requires every company that makes a GLP-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1 RA) to add information about NAION to the Warnings and Precautions section of the label.

The rule covers the whole class of drugs, including semaglutide, tirzepatide, dulaglutide, liraglutide, exenatide and lixisenatide. Combination products that pair a GLP-1 with insulin are covered too.

The FDA calls this a potential increased risk. The agency found more NAION cases in people taking GLP-1s than in people taking a placebo or another diabetes medicine, and cases have been reported with every GLP-1 currently on the market. But it still hasn't shown that the drugs cause NAION.

One reason is that diabetes, high blood pressure and sleep apnea all raise the risk of NAION, and many people who take GLP-1s have these conditions. The FDA also hasn't found a biological explanation for the link in animal studies.

The FDA added the warning anyway because the evidence kept pointing the same way. Its review of 126 clinical trials found a higher rate of NAION in people taking a GLP-1. Published studies of people with type 2 diabetes who started a GLP-1 found the same pattern when they were compared with people who started a DPP-4 inhibitor or an SGLT-2 inhibitor.

What Is NAION? The Optic Nerve Condition Behind the GLP-1 Eye Warning

NAION stands for nonarteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy. The optic nerve carries signals from your eye to your brain.

In NAION, blood flow to the front of that nerve drops, and the nerve tissue is damaged. Doctors sometimes call it an "eye stroke".

NAION usually affects one eye. The vision loss comes on suddenly and without pain, and many people first notice it when they wake up. About 4 in 10 people get some vision back, mostly within the first six months, but for others the loss is permanent.

Having NAION in one eye also matters for the other eye. The FDA notes that if NAION happens in one eye, the other eye may be at higher risk later. This is part of the reason the FDA advises people who have had NAION to talk to their doctor about whether to keep taking a GLP-1.

NAION is different from diabetic retinopathy, the eye disease most people with diabetes are screened for. Retinopathy damages the blood vessels in the retina and usually develops slowly over years. NAION affects the optic nerve and happens suddenly, so a normal retinopathy screening result doesn't rule it out.

Which GLP-1 Medicines the FDA NAION Warning Covers

The NAION warning applies to every GLP-1 receptor agonist approved in the US. That includes the weekly injections, the daily tablets, and combination products that pair a GLP-1 with long-acting insulin. If your medicine is in the table below, its label will carry the new warning.

The FDA's announcement doesn't mention compounded semaglutide or tirzepatide. These versions aren't FDA-approved, so they don't have an official label that can be updated. They contain the same active ingredient, though, so it makes sense to watch for the same symptoms if you use one.

How Rare Is NAION on GLP-1 Medicines? The Numbers From the FDA Review

NAION is rare, and it stays rare in people taking a GLP-1. The FDA pooled 126 clinical trials with more than 134,000 people, because single trials had too few cases to show anything.

Across all of those trials, there were only 15 probable NAION cases. Ten were in people taking a GLP-1 and five were in people taking a placebo.

In the trials, NAION occurred in about 6.4 people per 100,000 each year on a GLP-1, compared with 3.8 per 100,000 on a placebo or another diabetes medicine.

That works out to roughly two or three extra cases for every 100,000 people taking a GLP-1 for a year. Put another way, more than 99,990 people out of 100,000 would not develop NAION in a year on these medicines.

The FDA also looked at real-world health records through its Sentinel system. It followed adults with type 2 diabetes who started a GLP-1, an SGLT-2 inhibitor or a DPP-4 inhibitor between 2019 and 2024.

NAION rates were higher in this group than in the trials, at around 26 to 32 cases per 100,000 people each year. The rates were similar across all three types of diabetes medicine, which is part of the reason the FDA describes the link as possible rather than proven.

Some questions are still open. The FDA doesn't yet know whether the risk differs between individual GLP-1s, between doses, or between people taking them for diabetes and people taking them for weight. It has asked doctors to report NAION cases so it can keep tracking the risk.

NAION Symptoms on Ozempic, Mounjaro and Other GLP-1s: When to Get Same-Day Eye Care

NAION symptoms come on suddenly and usually affect only one eye. There's typically no pain, which is why people sometimes put it down to tiredness and wait. Many people first notice the change when they wake up in the morning.

Watch for these changes in one eye:

  • Sudden blurred or dim vision
  • A dark, blurry or missing area, often across the top or bottom half of what you see
  • Colors that look faded or washed out
  • Less sharp central vision, making it harder to read or recognize faces

If you notice any of these changes while taking a GLP-1, contact your doctor or an eye specialist the same day. Don't wait for it to clear up on its own, and don't wait for your next routine appointment. Your doctor will usually refer you to an ophthalmologist, who can examine the optic nerve and rule out other causes of sudden vision loss.

Some causes of sudden vision loss are emergencies. If the change in your vision comes with weakness, numbness, a drooping face or trouble speaking, call emergency services, because these can be signs of a stroke. The same applies if you lose vision in both eyes at once.

Who Has a Higher NAION Risk? Risk Factors That Overlap With GLP-1 Use

According to the FDA, the following can raise the chance of developing NAION:

  • Diabetes
  • High blood pressure
  • Sleep apnea
  • Kidney disease
  • Anemia
  • Smoking

Many of the people who take GLP-1 medicines already have one or more of these conditions.

Type 2 diabetes is the main reason GLP-1s are prescribed, and high blood pressure and sleep apnea are common in people living with obesity. This overlap is a large part of the reason it's hard to tell how much of the extra risk comes from the medicine and how much comes from the conditions it treats.

The shape of your eye matters too. Some people have a small optic disc, the spot where the optic nerve leaves the back of the eye, with the nerve fibers tightly packed together.

Eye specialists call this a "crowded" disc, and it's linked with NAION. An eye exam can show whether you have one.

If you're living with type 2 diabetes, keeping your blood pressure, blood sugar and sleep apnea treatment on track protects your eyes as well as your heart and kidneys. It also lowers several of the NAION risk factors above, whichever medicine you take.

Should You Stop Your GLP-1 Because of the NAION Warning?

Don't stop taking your GLP-1 because of this warning without talking to your prescriber first. The FDA hasn't told patients to stop. Its advice is to know the symptoms and get help quickly if your vision changes.

Stopping suddenly can raise your blood sugar and lead to weight regain, and for some people it means losing protection for the heart.

The advice changes if you've already had NAION. In that case, the FDA asks you to talk to your doctor about whether to keep taking the medicine. It tells doctors to consider stopping the GLP-1 unless the benefits clearly outweigh the risks, partly because NAION can come back and can affect the other eye.

If you haven't had NAION, the warning is a good reason to have a conversation, not to change your treatment on the spot. Your doctor can weigh your personal risk factors against what the medicine is doing for you. They may suggest an eye exam, especially if you have several risk factors or existing eye problems.

If you and your doctor do decide to switch or stop a diabetes medicine, closer glucose tracking helps you see the effect early. For people with type 2 diabetes who don't use insulin, a continuous glucose monitor can show how your blood sugar responds to a medication change day by day.

Questions to Ask Your Doctor About the GLP-1 Eye Risk

Your next appointment is a good time to talk through the NAION warning, and you don't need to wait for a vision problem to bring it up. These questions can help you get clear answers:

  1. Do I have any of the risk factors for NAION, and how much do they raise my chance?
  2. Do the benefits of my GLP-1 still outweigh this risk for me?
  3. Should I have an eye exam now, so there's a record of my optic nerve to compare with later?
  4. If my vision suddenly changes, who should I call first, and how quickly?
  5. If I ever need to stop this medicine, what would we use instead?

If you see an eye specialist, tell them you take a GLP-1 and which one. That way they can factor it into any exam.

How to Report a GLP-1 Side Effect to the FDA Through MedWatch

If you've had vision changes or another side effect while taking a GLP-1, you can report it to the FDA through its MedWatch program.

Patients, caregivers and health professionals can all file a report, online or by phone. Reports like these help the FDA track how often NAION happens and whether the risk differs between medicines.

If you live outside the US, report side effects to your national medicines regulator. Your pharmacist can tell you how.

Key Takeaway

The FDA now requires every GLP-1 medicine to carry a warning about NAION, a rare optic nerve condition that can cause sudden vision loss in one eye. The risk is small, at roughly two or three extra cases per 100,000 people a year, and the FDA hasn't proven the medicines cause it. Keep taking your GLP-1 unless your prescriber advises otherwise, and get same-day care if the vision in one eye suddenly changes.

GLP-1 NAION Warning: Frequently Asked Questions

Does Ozempic Cause Blindness?

Ozempic doesn't usually affect vision, and the FDA hasn't proven that it causes NAION. The FDA found a small possible increase in NAION risk across all GLP-1 medicines, including Ozempic. NAION can cause permanent vision loss in one eye, but it's rare, and total blindness in both eyes from NAION is very uncommon.

Is the NAION Risk the Same for Wegovy, Mounjaro and Zepbound?

The warning applies equally to every GLP-1 medicine, including Wegovy, Mounjaro and Zepbound. The FDA doesn't yet know whether the risk differs between individual drugs or doses. For now, the same advice applies to all of them.

Can NAION Vision Loss Be Reversed?

There's no proven treatment that reverses NAION. About 4 in 10 people get some vision back, usually within the first six months, but for others the loss is permanent. Getting checked quickly still matters, because a doctor needs to rule out other causes that can be treated.

Does the NAION Warning Apply to Compounded Semaglutide or Tirzepatide?

The FDA's announcement doesn't mention compounded versions, which aren't FDA-approved and don't have an official label. They contain the same active ingredients as the approved medicines. It's sensible to watch for the same eye symptoms if you use one.

Should I Get an Eye Exam Before Starting a GLP-1?

The FDA hasn't asked for an eye exam before starting a GLP-1. An exam can still be useful if you have risk factors such as diabetes, high blood pressure or sleep apnea, or existing eye problems. Ask your doctor whether a baseline exam makes sense for you.
