Type 2 Diabetes and Alzheimer's: What a New Review of 40 Studies Found

Have you ever walked into, let's say, your kitchen or bedroom and couldn't remember why you came in? It happens to more people than you'd imagine, and yes, age has a lot to do with it. But when you're living with type 2 diabetes, that forgetfulness may get you wondering if your blood sugar is affecting your memory.

A team at Curtin University in Australia pulled together 40 studies and found that people with type 2 diabetes were 53% more likely to develop Alzheimer's disease than people without diabetes. It's a worrying find, but the good news is that the real numbers are much smaller than the headline suggests.

Across the studies, about 5 in every 1,000 people with type 2 diabetes developed Alzheimer's each year. That's encouraging, because many of the habits that help you manage diabetes are also linked to a healthier brain.

Key Takeaways

  • In a review of 40 studies published in November 2026, people with type 2 diabetes had a 53% higher risk of Alzheimer's disease.
  • That 53% compares people with and without diabetes. In everyday numbers, about 5 in 1,000 people with type 2 diabetes developed Alzheimer's each year.
  • The studies show a link, but they weren't designed to prove that diabetes causes Alzheimer's.
  • The rise in risk was about the same for women and men.
  • Keeping your blood sugar and blood pressure in range and staying active are linked to a lower risk of dementia, and Alzheimer's is its most common form.

Type 2 Diabetes and Alzheimer's: Why So Many People Are Worried

If you have type 2 diabetes, you already keep an eye on a lot, from your blood sugar and your feet to your eyes and your kidneys. So when headlines start linking diabetes to Alzheimer's, it's natural to feel like one more worry has been added to the pile. Many people also start second-guessing every small memory slip, such as a missed appointment or a name on the tip of their tongue.

This link isn't new. For years, researchers who followed large groups of people over long periods have noticed that adults with type 2 diabetes seem more likely to develop Alzheimer's, the most common cause of dementia, as the Alzheimer's Association fact sheet on diabetes and cognitive decline explains.

You may also have come across the phrase "type 3 diabetes" online. In 2005, Dr. Suzanne de la Monte and her team at Brown University proposed the term in the Journal of Alzheimer's Disease after finding that brain cells in people with Alzheimer's seemed to respond poorly to insulin, much like muscle and liver cells do in type 2 diabetes. Type 3 diabetes isn't a medical diagnosis, and doctors don't use it to diagnose anyone. It's simply a label some researchers use to describe a possible link between insulin problems and Alzheimer's.

Having diabetes doesn't mean you'll go on to develop Alzheimer's. As the Alzheimer's Society guide to diabetes and dementia risk puts it, diabetes is a risk factor, and many people with diabetes never develop dementia. What the new review adds is a clearer picture of how often Alzheimer's actually happens in people with type 2 diabetes, which is far less often than the 53% headline suggests.

What the New Type 2 Diabetes and Alzheimer's Study Found

The new research is a review of studies other teams had already published, which researchers call a meta-analysis. Pooling studies this way gives a far clearer picture than any single study can. The team, led by researchers at Curtin University, published their findings in Alzheimer's Research and Therapy on November 9, 2026.

They began by searching four large medical databases for every study up to April 2026 that looked at type 2 diabetes and Alzheimer's. That search turned up 9,431 articles, and after checking each one, the team kept the 40 that met their standards. Of those, 23 studies tracked how many new Alzheimer's cases appeared, and 26 compared the risk in people with and without diabetes.

What sets this review apart is how narrow it is. Earlier reviews often grouped different types of diabetes together, or counted every kind of dementia as one. This team looked only at type 2 diabetes and only at Alzheimer's disease, so the results speak directly to people living with type 2 diabetes.

When the team pooled the results, people with type 2 diabetes had a 53% higher risk of developing Alzheimer's than people without diabetes. The researchers adjusted for other factors the original studies had measured, so the extra risk isn't simply explained by things like age. They also found that the results held up when they reran the numbers in different ways.

In Summary

This review is one of the largest looks yet at type 2 diabetes and Alzheimer's, and it focused only on those two conditions. It confirms that people with type 2 diabetes face a higher risk of Alzheimer's than people without diabetes. On its own, though, the 53% figure doesn't tell you how likely you are to develop it, and the next section explains why.

What a 53% Higher Alzheimer's Risk Means for People With Diabetes

A 53% higher risk sounds like you have a one-in-two chance of getting Alzheimer's, but that's not what the number means. It compares two groups of people, those with type 2 diabetes and those without, and tells you how much more often Alzheimer's shows up in the first group. Emmanuel Adewuyi, one of the study's authors, told Newsweek that the finding doesn't mean that someone with type 2 diabetes has a 53 percent chance of developing the disease.

Think of it like the odds of rain. If the chance of rain goes up from 2 days a month to 3, that's a 50% increase, yet most of the month still stays dry. Relative risk works the same way, because a big percentage rise on a small starting number still leaves you with a small number.

That's why the review's second finding is the one to hold on to. Across the studies, about 5 in every 1,000 people with type 2 diabetes developed Alzheimer's each year. Put another way, in a typical year, about 995 out of every 1,000 people with type 2 diabetes did not develop it.

The researchers also looked at women and men separately. Women had slightly more new cases each year, about 3.6 per 1,000 compared with 2.8 per 1,000 in men. The rise in risk linked to diabetes was almost the same for both, so neither women nor men with diabetes need to worry more than the other.

In Summary

The 53% figure tells you diabetes is linked to a higher risk, not that half of people with diabetes will get Alzheimer's. In real numbers, about 5 in 1,000 people with type 2 diabetes developed it each year. The extra risk linked to diabetes was about the same for women and men.

Does Diabetes Cause Alzheimer's? What the Research Can and Can't Tell Us

The short answer is that nobody knows yet. All 40 studies in the review were observational, which means researchers watched what happened to groups of people over time without changing anything about their care. Studies like these are good at spotting links, but they can't prove that one condition causes another, a point Adewuyi made himself.

Part of the reason is that type 2 diabetes rarely shows up alone. People with diabetes are also more likely to have high blood pressure, high cholesterol and extra weight, and each of these is linked to a higher risk of dementia, including Alzheimer's. The studies adjusted for many of these factors, but it's hard to fully separate diabetes from everything that tends to come with it.

The numbers also varied a lot from one study to the next. Studies that relied only on hospital or insurance records found about 4 new Alzheimer's cases per 1,000 people each year, while studies that checked people directly found about 10. Records can easily miss a diagnosis, so the true number may sit somewhere between the two.

The review also leaves some questions open. It didn't look at how the risk changes with how long someone has had diabetes, how well their blood sugar is controlled or which medicines they take, which are the details most people with diabetes would want to know. The paper was also released early, so its final version may still change slightly.

In Summary

This review shows that type 2 diabetes and Alzheimer's are linked, but it can't tell us that diabetes causes Alzheimer's. The real number of cases may be a little higher than 5 in 1,000, because medical records can miss diagnoses. We also don't yet know how factors such as good blood sugar control change the risk, so it's worth talking to your doctor about your own situation.

Why Type 2 Diabetes May Affect the Brain and Alzheimer's Risk

Scientists don't yet know exactly how diabetes and Alzheimer's are connected, but they have a few good leads. Most of them come back to two things you already know well from living with diabetes, which are insulin and blood sugar.

Insulin problems in the brain. Your brain uses insulin too, and not only to bring in sugar for energy. Insulin also helps brain cells talk to each other and form memories. A 2024 review on brain insulin resistance and Alzheimer's found signs that brain cells in people with Alzheimer's respond poorly to insulin, much like your muscles do in type 2 diabetes, and this may play a part in memory problems.

Damage to blood vessels. Over time, high blood sugar can harm blood vessels all over your body, which is why diabetes raises the risk of heart disease and stroke. Your brain relies on a dense web of tiny blood vessels, and when they're damaged, brain cells get less of the oxygen and fuel they need.

Inflammation. High blood sugar also sets off inflammation, the body's way of reacting to harm. When inflammation lingers for years, it may damage brain cells. One study also found that people with high blood sugar had much more beta-amyloid, a sticky protein that builds up in the brains of people with Alzheimer's.

None of these explanations is proven yet, and the real answer is likely a mix of all three. They also point to something hopeful. Each one is tied to blood sugar, blood pressure and blood vessel health, which are the same things you already work on every day to manage your diabetes.

How to Lower Alzheimer's Risk When You Have Diabetes

Here's the encouraging part. In 2024, a group of 27 dementia experts known as the Lancet Commission on dementia prevention found that nearly half of all dementia cases around the world could be prevented or delayed by tackling 14 risk factors. Diabetes is one of them, and so are several others that tend to come with it, such as high blood pressure, high LDL cholesterol, extra weight and not moving enough.

Alzheimer's is the most common type of dementia, so the habits that lower dementia risk overall help protect you from it too. You don't need to change everything at once. Each small win below helps your diabetes today and gives your brain some protection for later.

In Summary

Experts believe nearly half of dementia cases, including Alzheimer's, could be prevented or delayed. Many of the risk factors on their list are ones people with diabetes already work on. Managing your diabetes well is one of the best things you can do for your brain.

Keep Your Blood Sugar in Your Target Range

Since high blood sugar is linked to the inflammation and blood vessel damage described above, keeping it in range is your first line of defense. For many adults with diabetes, the American Diabetes Association Standards of Care suggest a fasting blood sugar of 80 to 130 mg/dL (4.4 to 7.2 mmol/L) and under 180 mg/dL (10.0 mmol/L) 1 to 2 hours after you start eating. Your doctor may set different targets for you, so check with them.

Try to avoid very low blood sugar as well. The Alzheimer's Association notes that people who had severe highs and lows were at higher risk of dementia, so the aim is steady numbers, not the lowest numbers. A continuous glucose monitor, even if you're not on insulin, can show you those highs and lows as they happen.

Know Your Blood Pressure Numbers

High blood pressure and diabetes often go together, and both can harm the small blood vessels in your brain. The Lancet Commission recommends keeping the top number of your blood pressure at 130 mm Hg or lower from around age 40. A home monitor makes it easy to keep track between doctor visits, and learning what causes high blood pressure and how it's treated can help you make sense of your readings.

Move After Meals

You don't need a gym membership for this one. A short walk after meals helps bring down the blood sugar rise that follows eating, and regular activity helps your heart, your weight and your mood too. Even a 10-minute walk after dinner counts.

Check Your LDL Cholesterol

High LDL cholesterol was added to the Lancet's list in 2024, and the experts recommend finding and treating it from around age 40. People with diabetes are more likely to have high cholesterol, so ask for a cholesterol test at your next checkup if you haven't had one in a while. If you're unsure about treatment, find out how a calcium scan can help you decide on a statin.

Protect Your Hearing and Eyesight, and Stay Connected

These may seem unrelated to diabetes, but the Lancet Commission lists untreated hearing loss, vision loss and social isolation as risk factors for dementia. Diabetes can affect your eyes, so keep up with your yearly eye exam, and get your hearing checked if you're turning up the TV more than you used to. Regular time with friends and family keeps your brain busy too.

Questions to Ask Your Doctor About Diabetes and Alzheimer's Risk

If this news has been on your mind, bring it up at your next appointment. Your doctor knows your health history, so they can tell you what the research means for you in a way no article can. These questions can help you start the conversation.

  1. Are my blood sugar targets right for my age and health?
  2. Have I had any blood sugar lows I might not have noticed?
  3. Is my blood pressure where it should be, and should I check it at home?
  4. When was my last cholesterol test, and is my LDL in a healthy range?
  5. Are my eye exam and hearing check up to date?
  6. I've noticed some memory slips lately. Is that worth looking into?

Write down anything you've noticed before your visit, such as forgetting appointments or losing track of conversations. It's easy to forget the details once you're in the room.

One Little Win

Tonight, take a 10-minute walk after dinner. It helps bring down your after-meal blood sugar, gets your blood flowing and gives you a few quiet minutes to yourself. Do it again tomorrow and you've started a habit that's good for your diabetes and your brain.

Frequently Asked Questions About Type 2 Diabetes and Alzheimer's

Is Alzheimer's Type 3 Diabetes?

No. "Type 3 diabetes" isn't a medical diagnosis. Some researchers use the term to describe how brain cells in people with Alzheimer's seem to respond poorly to insulin, much like the body does in type 2 diabetes.

Does Diabetes Cause Alzheimer's?

Researchers don't know yet. Studies show that people with type 2 diabetes are more likely to develop Alzheimer's, but they haven't proven that diabetes causes it. Shared risk factors such as high blood pressure may be part of the link.

Can Controlling Blood Sugar Lower Alzheimer's Risk?

Keeping your blood sugar in range helps protect your blood vessels and may reduce inflammation, both of which are linked to brain health. Experts list diabetes as one of 14 risk factors that, if tackled, could prevent or delay nearly half of dementia cases, including Alzheimer's.

Are Women With Diabetes at Higher Risk of Alzheimer's?

In the new review, women had slightly more new Alzheimer's cases each year than men. The extra risk linked to diabetes was about the same for both.

Should I Get My Memory Checked If I Have Diabetes?

Occasional forgetfulness is normal at any age. If memory slips are happening more often or getting in the way of daily life, talk to your doctor. They can check for common causes, including blood sugar swings, before looking further.
