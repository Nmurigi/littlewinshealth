New Hormone Therapy for Early Breast Cancer: What the FDA's Wilserdly Approval Means for You

9 min read · November 2026 · By Anne M.

You finished surgery, maybe radiation too, and your oncologist handed you a prescription for a daily pill you'll take for the next five years. Each refill comes with the same quiet question in the back of your mind, which is what happens if the cancer comes back anyway.

If you've had estrogen receptor-positive breast cancer, or someone you love has, that worry is very common. For more than 25 years, the pills offered to answer it have been the same ones, tamoxifen or an aromatase inhibitor.

That changed on November 23, 2026. The FDA approved Wilserdly (giredestrant), the first new hormone therapy for early-stage breast cancer in over 20 years. In a large trial, it lowered the chance of the cancer returning more than the standard pills did.

Below, we explain who this new pill is for, what the study found, the side effects to know about and the questions to bring to your next appointment.

Key Takeaways

  • Wilserdly (giredestrant) is the first new hormone therapy for early breast cancer in over 20 years, approved by the FDA on November 23, 2026.
  • It's a daily pill taken after surgery by adults with ER-positive, HER2-negative stage I to III breast cancer at intermediate to high risk of coming back.
  • In a trial of 4,170 people, it lowered the chance of the cancer returning, or of death, by 30% compared with tamoxifen or an aromatase inhibitor.
  • It's too early to know if it helps people live longer.
  • Ask your oncologist if it fits your situation, and don't stop your current pill without talking to them first.

How Common Is Breast Cancer, and How Many Cases Are ER-Positive?

Breast cancer is the most common cancer in American women apart from skin cancer, and it accounts for about 1 in 3 new cancers in women each year. The American Cancer Society's 2026 breast cancer statistics estimate that about 321,910 women in the US will be diagnosed with invasive breast cancer this year.

Men get it too, though far less often. About 2,670 men are expected to be diagnosed in 2026, and the new approval covers men as well as women.

Most of these cancers are a specific type. About 70% of breast cancers are estrogen receptor-positive and HER2-negative, according to the FDA's announcement of the Wilserdly approval. That's the type the new drug treats.

Here's what those terms mean in everyday words.

  • Estrogen receptor-positive (ER-positive) means the cancer cells use the hormone estrogen to grow. Blocking estrogen, or the receptor it attaches to, can slow or stop them.
  • HER2-negative means the cells don't make high levels of a growth protein called HER2, so drugs that target HER2 won't help.

The good news is that most breast cancers are found early. About 66% are diagnosed while still confined to the breast, when treatment tends to work best, according to Breastcancer.org's breast cancer facts and statistics. Even so, about 42,140 American women are expected to die of breast cancer in 2026.

Why ER-Positive Breast Cancer Recurrence Risk Lasts for Years

ER-positive breast cancer can come back long after treatment ends, sometimes 15 or 20 years later. That's why doctors don't stop at surgery and prescribe a daily hormone pill afterward.

For decades, that pill has been one of two kinds. Tamoxifen blocks estrogen from attaching to the cancer cells, and aromatase inhibitors (anastrozole, letrozole and exemestane) lower how much estrogen the body makes. Most people take one of them for about five years.

These pills work well. Five years of tamoxifen alone cuts the risk of the cancer coming back by about half, according to a review of ER-positive breast cancer after adjuvant endocrine therapy. But they don't remove the risk completely.

The clearest picture comes from a large analysis by the Early Breast Cancer Trialists' Collaborative Group. The researchers pooled 88 trials with 62,923 women who had ER-positive breast cancer, all of whom took hormone therapy for five years and were cancer-free when they stopped. Over the next 15 years, the cancer kept spreading to other parts of the body in a small but constant share of these women, as late as 20 years after diagnosis, as Michigan Medicine reported on the 20-year recurrence study.

The risk wasn't the same for everyone. It depended on how large the original tumor was and how many lymph nodes had cancer in them. Women whose lymph nodes were clear still had a 13% to 19% chance of the cancer spreading over those 15 years, CBS News reported.

These numbers sound frightening, but they come with real progress. Some women now take hormone pills for longer than five years, which lowers the risk further, and doctors use tumor size, lymph nodes and genetic tests to decide who needs more treatment.

This is the problem Wilserdly was tested against. The trial asked if a newer hormone pill could stop more cancers from coming back than tamoxifen or an aromatase inhibitor.

In Summary

ER-positive breast cancer can return many years after you finish your hormone pills, and the risk is higher with larger tumors or cancer in the lymph nodes. Tamoxifen and aromatase inhibitors lower that risk a lot, but not to zero. That leftover risk is what a new hormone therapy for early breast cancer is trying to reduce.

What the FDA Approved: Wilserdly (Giredestrant) for Early-Stage Breast Cancer

On November 23, 2026, the FDA approved Wilserdly, a daily pill whose generic name is giredestrant, for some people with early-stage breast cancer. It's made by Genentech, part of the Roche Group, and it went through Priority Review, the FDA's faster route for drugs that may be a real improvement on existing treatment.

Wilserdly is taken after surgery to lower the chance of the cancer coming back. In medical terms this is called adjuvant therapy, and it does the same job tamoxifen and aromatase inhibitors have done for decades.

Who Can Take Wilserdly After Breast Cancer Surgery

The approval covers adults with ER-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer at stage I, II or III whose cancer has an intermediate to high risk of coming back. Your oncologist judges that risk from details like tumor size, lymph nodes, grade and genetic test results.

Wilserdly isn't meant for everyone with early breast cancer. If your cancer is small, low grade and low risk, standard hormone pills may still be the better fit, and your care team can explain which group you're in.

How Wilserdly Works Compared With Tamoxifen

Wilserdly is a selective estrogen receptor degrader, or SERD. Tamoxifen blocks the estrogen receptor on cancer cells, and aromatase inhibitors lower how much estrogen the body makes. Wilserdly does something different. It blocks the receptor and also breaks it down, so the cancer cell loses the part it uses to respond to estrogen.

Other SERD pills are already sold for advanced breast cancer, but Wilserdly is the first one shown to help people after surgery for early-stage disease, as BioPharma Dive reported on the Wilserdly approvals.

How You Take It

Wilserdly is a pill you swallow once a day. Women who haven't been through menopause also need a second medicine that switches off hormone production in the ovaries. Some men take that added medicine too, depending on what their doctor decides.

What the lidERA Trial Found About Giredestrant vs Standard Hormone Therapy

People who took giredestrant after surgery were 30% less likely to have their cancer come back, or to die, than people who took tamoxifen or an aromatase inhibitor. That's the main result of lidERA, the trial behind the approval. The FDA's decision rests on it.

Who Was in the Trial

The trial included 4,170 adults with ER-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer at stage I to III and an intermediate to high risk of recurrence. Everyone had already had surgery, and those who needed chemotherapy had finished it, according to the lidERA results presented at the San Antonio Breast Cancer Symposium.

Their median age was 54, and a little over half (59%) had been through menopause. About 41% were premenopausal, so the results cover younger women too.

Half the group took giredestrant once a day. The other half took standard hormone therapy, either tamoxifen or an aromatase inhibitor (anastrozole, letrozole or exemestane). Both groups were meant to stay on treatment for up to five years.

The Main Results After About 3 Years

The results come from a planned early check, about 2 years and 8 months into the trial on average, as the ASCO Post's report on the lidERA trial describes. At that point, 140 people taking giredestrant had their cancer come back or had died, compared with 196 people on standard therapy.

At the 3-year mark, the results looked like this.

  • 92.4% of people taking giredestrant were alive with no invasive breast cancer
  • 89.6% of people on standard hormone pills were alive with no invasive breast cancer

That difference works out to about 28 more people out of every 1,000 still free of invasive cancer after 3 years. People on giredestrant were also less likely to have the cancer spread to distant parts of the body.

Results Before and After Menopause

The benefit showed up in both groups, according to updated lidERA results shared at ASCO 2026. In people who hadn't been through menopause, giredestrant lowered the risk by 35%, and 94.0% were cancer-free at 3 years compared with 91.5% on standard pills. In people after menopause, the risk dropped by about 26%, with 91.3% compared with 88.3%.

Fewer People Stopped Taking It

Hormone pills only help if you keep taking them, and many people stop early because of side effects like joint pain or hot flashes. In lidERA, 347 people stopped giredestrant compared with 520 who stopped standard therapy, which suggests many people found it easier to stay on.

What the Trial Hasn't Shown Yet

It's too early to know if giredestrant helps people live longer. The early survival numbers leaned toward giredestrant, but the difference was small enough that it could be down to chance, as Targeted Oncology reported on the interim survival data. The FDA has asked the company to send in the survival results once the trial has run longer.

Three years is also a short window for a cancer that can return 15 or 20 years later. Doctors will be watching how the results hold up over time.

In Summary

In a trial of more than 4,000 people, giredestrant stopped more early breast cancers from coming back than tamoxifen or an aromatase inhibitor, and fewer people quit taking it. After 3 years, about 28 more people in every 1,000 were still free of invasive cancer. We don't yet know if it helps people live longer, so this is a promising result that still needs longer follow-up.

Giredestrant Side Effects to Know About

Most side effects of giredestrant are the kind people already know from hormone pills. In the lidERA trial, the most common ones in both groups were joint pain, hot flashes and headache, and they were mostly mild, according to the American Association for Cancer Research summary of lidERA.

According to the FDA, side effects that happened in at least 1 in 10 people taking Wilserdly include the following.

  • Muscle and joint pain
  • Hot flashes
  • Tiredness
  • Headache
  • Nausea
  • A slower than normal heart rate
  • Dizziness
  • Numbness or tingling in the hands and feet

Blood tests can change too. Some people had raised liver enzymes, lower blood counts, higher blood fats, or changes in mineral levels and kidney results, so your care team will generally check your blood while you're on it.

Heart Rate and Pregnancy Warnings

Two warnings stand out. The first is a slow heart rate, which the Wilserdly prescribing information lists as a warning. Tell your doctor if you have a heart rhythm problem, take medicine that slows your heart, or notice dizziness, fainting or unusual tiredness.

The second is pregnancy. Wilserdly can harm a developing baby, so if you could become pregnant, ask your doctor about birth control while you take it and for a while after you stop.

If side effects make it hard to keep going, talk to your care team before you stop. Many side effects can be eased, and switching pills is often possible.

Early vs Advanced Breast Cancer: Two Wilserdly Approvals in One Month

If you've seen Wilserdly in the news more than once this month, there's a reason. The FDA approved it twice in November 2026, for two very different groups of people.

The first approval came on November 16, for advanced breast cancer, meaning cancer that has spread or can't be removed with surgery. In that case Wilserdly is taken with another cancer pill called everolimus. It's only for people whose cancer kept growing or came back despite earlier hormone therapy and whose tumor carries a change in a gene called ESR1.

ESR1 changes help cancer cells get around hormone pills, and up to 40% of people with this type of advanced breast cancer develop them, according to HealthDay's report on the advanced breast cancer approval. A blood test approved by the FDA checks for the change before treatment starts.

In the evERA trial behind that approval, people with the ESR1 change who took Wilserdly with everolimus went a median of 10 months before their cancer got worse. People on standard hormone therapy with everolimus went 5.5 months, as Breastcancer.org explains in its giredestrant overview.

The second approval, on November 23, is the one this article covers. It's for early-stage cancer after surgery, Wilserdly is taken alone, and no gene test is needed.

Questions to Ask Your Oncologist About Hormone Therapy After Breast Cancer Surgery

Wilserdly is brand new, so your care team may still be working out where it fits. These questions can help you have that talk at your next appointment.

  • Is my recurrence risk intermediate or high? The approval only covers people in those groups, and your risk depends on your tumor size, lymph nodes, grade and genetic test results.
  • Should I switch if I'm already on tamoxifen or an aromatase inhibitor? The trial tested giredestrant as the first hormone pill after surgery. It didn't test switching partway through, so ask what your doctor thinks in your case.
  • Will I need a hormone-suppressing medicine as well? If you haven't been through menopause, the answer is generally yes. Ask what that involves and how often you'd need it.
  • How will we watch my heart rate and blood tests? Ask how often you'll have checkups and which symptoms you should call about.
  • What if I'm planning a pregnancy? Wilserdly can harm a developing baby, so this conversation should happen before you start.
  • Will my insurance cover it, and what will I pay? New cancer drugs can be expensive, and it can take time before insurers add them to their lists. Ask if your clinic has a financial counselor or can help you apply for a patient support program.

Write down your answers or bring someone with you. Appointments move fast, and it's easy to forget what was said.

What This New Breast Cancer Treatment Means for You

If you have ER-positive, HER2-negative early breast cancer with an intermediate or high risk of coming back, there's now a second kind of hormone pill to discuss with your oncologist. In a large trial it stopped more cancers from returning than the pills doctors have used for more than 25 years, and fewer people stopped taking it.

If your current hormone pill is working and you're managing the side effects, there's no need to stop it or change anything on your own. Bring this approval up at your next appointment, ask where your own risk sits, and decide on a plan together.

Frequently Asked Questions About Wilserdly and Early Breast Cancer

What Is Wilserdly?

Wilserdly is the brand name for giredestrant, a pill taken once a day for ER-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer. It belongs to a group of drugs called selective estrogen receptor degraders (SERDs), which block the estrogen receptor on cancer cells and break it down. The FDA approved it for early-stage breast cancer after surgery on November 23, 2026.

Is Giredestrant Better Than Tamoxifen?

In the lidERA trial, people taking giredestrant were 30% less likely to have their cancer come back, or to die, than people on tamoxifen or an aromatase inhibitor. After 3 years, 92.4% were free of invasive cancer compared with 89.6%. We don't know yet if it helps people live longer, and the best choice for you depends on your risk level and health.

Who Can Take Wilserdly for Early Breast Cancer?

It's approved for adults who've had surgery for ER-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer at stage I, II or III with an intermediate to high risk of recurrence. Women who haven't been through menopause also need a medicine that suppresses hormone production, and some men take one too.

What Are the Side Effects of Giredestrant?

The most common side effects are muscle and joint pain, hot flashes, tiredness, headache, nausea, a slow heart rate, dizziness, and numbness or tingling in the hands and feet. It can also change some blood test results, and it can harm a developing baby if taken during pregnancy.

Can Breast Cancer Come Back After Hormone Therapy?

Yes. ER-positive breast cancer can return many years after five years of hormone pills, sometimes 15 to 20 years after diagnosis. The risk is higher with larger tumors or cancer in the lymph nodes, and longer or newer hormone therapies aim to lower it further.
