Normal Blood Sugar Levels by Age: What the Numbers Really Mean

11 min read · August 2026 · Little Wins Health

You checked your blood sugar, saw a number, and now you want to know one thing, if it's normal for someone your age.

That question sends most people straight to a chart like this one, and it deserves a clear answer rather than a wall of figures. So here's the short version first, with the detail underneath it.

Here's the part most charts quietly skip. The line for a normal reading doesn't move as you get older, and a normal fasting number is the same at 30, 50, and 75. Something does change with age, but it isn't what the usual "by age" tables suggest, and we'll get to why that trips so many people up.

The Short Answer

For a healthy adult at any age, these are the normal ranges:

  • Fasting: 70 to 99 mg/dL (3.9 to 5.5 mmol/L)
  • Two hours after eating: under 140 mg/dL (7.8 mmol/L)
  • A1C, your three-month average: below 5.7%

The One Thing Most Charts Get Wrong About Age

Type "blood sugar by age" into a search bar and you'll find tables that show the normal range creeping up decade by decade. That framing is misleading, and it worries a lot of people for no reason.

The cutoff that defines a normal reading is the same at every adult age. A fasting level of 90 mg/dL (5.0 mmol/L) is normal at 25 and normal at 70, full stop. What actually changes with age is the treatment target set for people who already have diabetes, which is a different thing entirely.

For many older adults, doctors deliberately ease that target upward. The reason is safety, since a low blood sugar becomes more dangerous with age, bringing a higher risk of falls and confusion. The American Diabetes Association 2026 guidelines now set these targets individually, based on age, other health conditions, and frailty rather than age alone.

So when a chart shows "higher normal levels" for older people, it's really showing a relaxed management goal for someone with diabetes, not a new definition of normal. Keep that distinction in mind and the rest of the numbers fall into place.

One reading on its own rarely settles anything. A number in the prediabetes or diabetes range generally needs a repeat test on a separate day before it means what it looks like, which we'll come back to.

Fasting Blood Sugar, Explained

Fasting means no food or calorie-containing drinks for at least 8 hours, so it's generally the reading you take first thing in the morning. It shows what your body settles to when nothing has been eaten, which makes it a clean baseline.

A normal fasting level runs from 70 to 99 mg/dL (3.9 to 5.5 mmol/L). Once you reach 100 to 125 mg/dL (5.6 to 6.9 mmol/L), that's the prediabetes range, and 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) or higher on repeat testing points to diabetes.

This is where the common worry about a reading of 110 comes in. A fasting 110 mg/dL (6.1 mmol/L) sits in the prediabetes range, not the normal one, so it's worth a conversation with your doctor rather than a panic. Prediabetes is often the point where small changes make the biggest difference.

Mornings can also read a little higher than you'd expect, thanks to a natural hormone surge before dawn that nudges your liver to release glucose. It's called the dawn phenomenon, and a slightly raised morning number can be normal because of it.

Blood Sugar After Eating

Your blood sugar is supposed to rise after a meal. Food turns into glucose, glucose enters your blood, and a healthy body brings the level back down within a couple of hours.

Two hours after eating, a normal reading is under 140 mg/dL (7.8 mmol/L). A formal reading of 140 to 199 mg/dL (7.8 to 11.0 mmol/L) at the two-hour mark of a glucose tolerance test falls in the prediabetes range, and 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) or higher points to diabetes.

That said, a single high number after a large, carb-heavy meal isn't a diagnosis. Home readings bounce around with what you ate and when you measured, so it's the pattern over days that tells the real story, not one spike.

Where Normal, Prediabetes, and Diabetes Divide

The three ranges aren't arbitrary. They mark points where the risk of long-term harm changes enough that doctors act differently, and the same tests are used to spot prediabetes and to diagnose diabetes.

Prediabetes is the middle ground, a signal that glucose is higher than it should be but not yet in the diabetes range. It generally causes no symptoms at all, which is exactly why the numbers count, and understanding what causes diabetes in the first place helps make sense of why they climb.

There's one exception to the repeat-testing rule. If you have clear symptoms of high blood sugar, such as heavy thirst, frequent urination, and unexpected weight loss, a random reading of 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) or higher is enough on its own to point to diabetes and needs prompt medical attention.

Blood Sugar and Age: What Actually Changes

Now we can put the age question to rest properly. For children, teens, and adults without diabetes, the normal glucose range is close to identical, with newborns being the one group that naturally runs a little lower.

The real shift comes for older adults who already have diabetes. Diabetes becomes far more common with age, and by 65 nearly 1 in 3 adults has it, which is why so much age-specific advice exists in the first place.

For those adults, a healthy older person might aim for an A1C below 7.0 to 7.5 percent, while someone with several other conditions might have a target below 8.0 percent, and someone in poor health a looser one still. That's a personalized safety decision, not a change in what a normal reading is.

What Your A1C Adds

A single glucose reading is a snapshot. Your A1C is the wider picture, an estimate of your average blood sugar over the past two to three months, taken from one blood draw with no fasting required.

Below 5.7 percent is normal, 5.7 to 6.4 percent is prediabetes, and 6.5 percent or higher points to diabetes. The useful trick is translating that percentage back into the everyday numbers you see on a meter.

What Makes a Reading Jump

If your numbers look different from one check to the next, that's normal, and it's not the meter failing you. Plenty of everyday things move blood sugar around, and these include:

  • Food, especially refined carbs and sugary drinks, which raise glucose fastest.
  • Stress and illness, since stress hormones push blood sugar up even without eating.
  • Poor sleep and the dawn phenomenon, both of which can lift morning readings.
  • Exercise, dehydration, and some medicines such as steroids, which each pull the number in their own direction.

Timing and method are just as important. A finger-stick sample from a home meter measures slightly differently from a lab blood draw, so a small gap between the two is expected rather than a mistake. Getting a clean, consistent reading is easier with a reliable home glucose meter and the same routine each time.

When a Number Means Call Your Doctor

Most readings just need noting, not acting on. A few are worth a call, and a rare few need same-day care. Book a test with your doctor if:

  • Your fasting readings sit at 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) or above more than once.
  • Any reading tops 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) alongside thirst, frequent urination, or blurred vision.
  • You get repeated lows under 70 mg/dL (3.9 mmol/L), which carry their own risks.

Treat it as urgent, though, if a very high reading, generally above 250 to 300 mg/dL (13.9 to 16.7 mmol/L), comes with nausea, vomiting, or fruity-smelling breath. Those can signal that the body is producing ketones, which is a medical emergency and needs care straight away.

Final Thoughts

Strip the age question back and the answer is simple. A normal blood sugar reading is defined by the same numbers at 25 or 75, and the "by age" charts are really describing management targets for people who already have diabetes.

Any single number is a snapshot, so the pattern over time is what counts, and your A1C fills in the rest. If a reading lands in the prediabetes range, take it as an early, useful nudge rather than a verdict.

One little win: tomorrow morning, take a single fasting reading before you eat or drink anything, and write it down. That one number, checked calmly, is the honest starting point. If it lands higher than you hoped, don't read a diagnosis into it, since one result never confirms anything on its own. Check again on another morning, and if a fasting reading sits at 100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L) or above more than once, book a test with your doctor to confirm what it means.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a fasting blood sugar of 110 normal?

No. A fasting reading of 110 mg/dL (6.1 mmol/L) sits in the prediabetes range, which runs from 100 to 125 mg/dL (5.6 to 6.9 mmol/L). It's not a diabetes diagnosis, but it's a good reason to talk with your doctor, since prediabetes is often reversible with early changes.

Is 140 after eating normal?

Two hours after eating, a normal level is under 140 mg/dL (7.8 mmol/L), so 140 sits right at the edge. On a formal glucose tolerance test, 140 to 199 mg/dL (7.8 to 11.0 mmol/L) is the prediabetes range. A one-off reading at home after a big meal isn't a diagnosis, though, so watch the pattern rather than a single number.

What should my blood sugar be first thing in the morning?

A normal fasting reading, taken before food or drink, is 70 to 99 mg/dL (3.9 to 5.5 mmol/L). Mornings can run slightly higher because of the dawn phenomenon, a natural hormone rise before you wake, so a small bump can still be normal.

Does normal blood sugar really stay the same with age?

Yes, for the definition of a normal reading. The cutoffs are the same for younger and older adults. What changes is the treatment target for people who already have diabetes, which doctors often ease for older adults to lower the risk of a dangerous low.

What is a normal blood sugar level in mmol/L?

A normal fasting level is 3.9 to 5.5 mmol/L, and under 7.8 mmol/L two hours after eating. These are the same values shown in mg/dL elsewhere on this page, just in the units used across the UK and much of Europe.
