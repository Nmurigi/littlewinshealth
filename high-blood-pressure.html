High Blood Pressure: What It Is, What Causes It and How to Treat It

The nurse loosens the cuff, glances at the screen and says your blood pressure is a little high. You felt fine walking in, so the number comes as a surprise, and now you're wondering what it means for your heart, your health and your plans for the rest of the week.

High blood pressure is one of the most common health problems in adults, and it's also one of the most treatable. Half of American adults have high blood pressure, and many of them don't know it, since it rarely causes symptoms until it's been high for years.

This guide explains what high blood pressure is, what your numbers mean, what causes it and how doctors treat it.

What to Know First

  • High blood pressure, also called hypertension, means the force of blood against your artery walls stays higher than it should.
  • A reading of 130/80 mmHg or higher counts as high blood pressure under the current US guideline.
  • One high reading isn't a diagnosis. Your doctor will want several readings taken on different days.
  • Most people have no symptoms, so regular checks are the only way to know.
  • Lifestyle changes and medication both lower blood pressure, and many people use the two together.
  • A reading of 180/120 or higher with chest pain, a severe headache, vision changes or trouble speaking needs emergency care.

What Is High Blood Pressure?

Blood pressure is the push of blood against the walls of your arteries as your heart pumps it around your body. High blood pressure means that push stays too forceful for too long, so your heart and blood vessels work harder than they should.

Every reading has two numbers, written like 120/80 mmHg. The top number, called systolic pressure, is the pressure in your arteries when your heart beats and pushes blood out. The bottom number, called diastolic pressure, is the pressure between beats, while your heart rests and fills with blood.

Your blood pressure rises and falls through the day, going up when you climb stairs, feel stressed or drink coffee and dropping when you sleep. That's normal. It becomes high blood pressure when your readings stay high even while you're resting.

Over time, that extra pressure strains the lining of your arteries and makes your heart pump harder with every beat.

Is Hypertension the Same as High Blood Pressure?

Yes. Hypertension is the medical name for high blood pressure, so you'll see it in your health records and hear it from your doctor. The word has nothing to do with stress or feeling tense. It simply means raised pressure in your arteries.

Blood Pressure Stages: What Your Numbers Mean

Doctors sort blood pressure into four stages. The 2025 AHA/ACC high blood pressure guideline kept the same cutoffs that US doctors have used since 2017.

If your two numbers fall into different stages, the higher stage counts. A reading of 126/84, for example, puts you in stage 1, because the bottom number is in the stage 1 range even though the top number is only elevated.

Elevated blood pressure isn't hypertension yet, but it's an early warning. It's the stage where small changes in your daily routine have the best chance of bringing your numbers back to normal.

Stage 1 and stage 2 both count as high blood pressure. The higher your stage, the more likely your doctor is to suggest medication alongside lifestyle changes.

One reading on its own doesn't put you in a stage. Your doctor will base a diagnosis on the average of several readings taken on at least two different days, so a single high number at the pharmacy is a reason to recheck rather than worry.

High Blood Pressure Symptoms

Most people with high blood pressure have no symptoms at all, which is why it's often called a silent killer. You can feel completely well while the extra pressure slowly strains your heart, blood vessels and kidneys, so checking your numbers is the only way to know.

Many signs people link to high blood pressure turn out to be unrelated. The American Heart Association's guide to high blood pressure symptoms explains that facial flushing, sweating, nervousness and trouble sleeping aren't reliable signs. The best evidence also shows that high blood pressure doesn't generally cause headaches or nosebleeds.

Dizziness is different. It can be a side effect of some blood pressure medicines, but sudden dizziness with loss of balance or trouble walking can be a sign of stroke and needs urgent care.

Symptoms are more likely when blood pressure climbs very high, around 180/120 or above. If you get a reading that high with a severe headache, chest pain, vision changes, weakness or trouble speaking, call 911.

What Causes High Blood Pressure?

High blood pressure generally comes from a mix of factors that build over years, including your genes, your age, your daily habits and sometimes another health condition. Doctors split it into two types depending on whether they can find a single cause.

Primary Hypertension

Most adults with high blood pressure have primary hypertension, sometimes called essential hypertension. It develops slowly over many years, and there's no single cause your doctor can point to.

Instead, several small pressures add up. Your arteries generally stiffen a little with age, your genes play a part, and habits like eating a lot of salt or sitting for most of the day push your numbers up over time.

That slow build is why many people are surprised by a high reading. Nothing changed overnight, but years of gradual rises finally crossed the line.

Secondary Hypertension

Secondary hypertension has a specific cause, such as another health condition or a medicine. It accounts for up to 10% of adults with high blood pressure, and treating the cause can sometimes bring blood pressure down or even back to normal.

Common causes include:

  • Kidney disease, the most common cause, since your kidneys help control the fluid and salt in your blood
  • Primary aldosteronism, a condition where the adrenal glands make too much of a hormone that holds on to salt
  • Obstructive sleep apnea, where your breathing stops and starts during sleep
  • Thyroid problems and, more rarely, other hormone conditions
  • Some medicines, including anti-inflammatory painkillers like ibuprofen, decongestants, steroids, some birth control pills and some antidepressants

Your doctor may look for a secondary cause if your blood pressure rises suddenly after being well controlled, stays high despite three or more medicines, or shows up before age 30 without the usual risk factors. Loud snoring with daytime sleepiness can also point toward sleep apnea.

Risk Factors You Can and Can't Change

Some risk factors are part of who you are. The American Heart Association's list of high blood pressure risk factors includes:

  • Age, since blood vessels lose some of their stretch over time
  • Family history, if a parent or close relative has high blood pressure
  • Sex, as men are more likely to develop it until age 64 and women are more likely from age 65
  • Race, as Black adults in the US develop high blood pressure more often, and it tends to be more severe
  • Chronic kidney disease, which can both cause high blood pressure and be worsened by it

Others are part of daily life, and these are where you have room to act:

  • Eating a lot of salt, especially from processed and restaurant food
  • Not moving much during the day
  • Carrying extra weight, which makes your heart and blood vessels work harder
  • Drinking alcohol regularly
  • Smoking or using tobacco
  • Long-term stress, which can raise blood pressure directly and make other healthy habits harder to keep
  • Untreated sleep apnea

High blood pressure also tends to travel with other conditions. Most people with diabetes develop high blood pressure, so if a test has shown prediabetes or high blood sugar, it's worth checking your blood pressure regularly too. You can also read more about how type 2 diabetes develops.

None of these factors means you'll definitely get high blood pressure, and having several doesn't mean you've done anything wrong. They tell you and your doctor where to look and which changes will help most.

High Blood Pressure in Young Adults

Many people assume that high blood pressure occurs only in older people, but that is not the case. In the latest US survey, nearly 1 in 4 adults aged 18 to 39 had hypertension, including about 3 in 10 young men and about 1 in 6 young women.

The bigger concern is how few of them know. Only about 27% of young adults with high blood pressure were aware they had it, compared with most older adults, partly because people in their 20s and 30s rarely get their blood pressure checked unless they're sick.

In most young adults, the causes are the same everyday factors that raise blood pressure later in life:

  • Weight gain, especially across the late teens and 20s
  • Salty processed food and takeaway meals, which make up a large part of many young people's diets
  • Long hours of sitting, at a desk, in a car or on a screen
  • Drinking and smoking, including vaping
  • Short or poor sleep, often linked to stress, shift work or late nights
  • Family history, which raises your risk at any age

When a Doctor Looks for Another Cause

A higher share of young people have secondary hypertension, where a separate condition drives the high readings. Research on adults aged 19 to 40 found that up to 10% of cases in this group are secondary, most often from hormone, kidney or heart conditions.

Your doctor is more likely to look for a hidden cause if your blood pressure is high before age 30 without the usual risk factors, if it's very high, or if it doesn't come down with treatment. Tests might include blood and urine checks for kidney function and hormone levels, a heart tracing (ECG), or a scan of the kidneys.

Finding high blood pressure young is good news in one sense, because you have decades to bring it down and protect your heart. If you're in your 20s or 30s, getting a reading at least once a year at a clinic or pharmacy is a simple habit that can catch it early.

How Hypertension Is Diagnosed

Your doctor diagnoses high blood pressure from the average of several readings, never from one number alone. Under the 2025 guideline, that means at least two careful readings on at least two separate occasions, because blood pressure moves up and down so much during the day.

A good clinic reading follows a set routine. You sit quietly for about five minutes with your back supported, feet flat on the floor and arm resting at heart level, and the cuff goes on your bare upper arm.

Home Blood Pressure Monitoring

Many doctors now ask you to check your blood pressure at home before confirming a diagnosis. Home readings show your normal numbers away from the stress of a clinic, and the American Heart Association's home monitoring advice recommends:

  • An upper-arm cuff, since wrist and finger monitors are less reliable
  • Two or three readings a minute apart, in the morning before medicine or food and again in the evening
  • No caffeine, exercise or smoking for 30 minutes before you measure
  • A written or app record of every reading to share with your doctor

24-Hour Blood Pressure Monitoring

Sometimes your doctor will ask you to wear a monitor for a full day and night. A cuff on your arm inflates every 15 to 30 minutes while you go about your normal day, and it keeps measuring while you sleep.

This test, called ambulatory blood pressure monitoring, catches patterns a clinic reading can miss. Some people have white coat hypertension, where their blood pressure rises only at the doctor's office. Others have masked hypertension, where it looks normal in the clinic but runs high at home or work.

Can a Smartwatch Measure Blood Pressure?

Not accurately enough for a diagnosis yet. The 2025 guideline advises against relying on cuffless devices, including smartwatches and rings, until they're shown to be more precise. If your watch flags a high reading, check it with a validated cuff before you worry.

How to Treat High Blood Pressure

High blood pressure is treated with lifestyle changes, medication or both, and for most adults the goal is a reading below 130/80. Your plan depends on your stage and your overall risk of heart disease and stroke.

Under the 2025 guideline, your doctor will generally follow this approach:

  • Elevated blood pressure, lifestyle changes on their own
  • Stage 1, lower risk, lifestyle changes first, with medication added if your blood pressure is still 130/80 or higher after 3 to 6 months
  • Stage 1 with diabetes, kidney disease, heart disease or a higher 10-year risk, medication and lifestyle changes together from the start
  • Stage 2, medication and lifestyle changes together from the start

To work out your 10-year risk, your doctor uses a tool called PREVENT, which looks at your age, cholesterol, blood sugar, kidney function and other health details.

Lifestyle Changes

Lifestyle changes lower blood pressure for everyone, including people who take tablets, and the effects add up when you combine them. The guideline recommends:

  • Eating less salt, ideally under 1,500 mg of sodium a day and no more than 2,300 mg
  • Trying a potassium-based salt substitute, unless you have kidney disease or take medicines that raise potassium
  • Following a DASH-style eating pattern, rich in vegetables, fruit, beans and low-fat dairy
  • Moving more, with a mix of aerobic and strength exercise through the week, and even a 10-minute walk after dinner counts
  • Drinking less alcohol, or none at all
  • Losing weight if you're carrying extra
  • Managing stress, with options such as meditation and breathing exercises

You don't need to change everything at once, since small changes that lower blood pressure add up over time.

Medication

If you need medication, it'll most likely come from one of the main first-choice groups. The American Heart Association's guide to blood pressure medications explains how each one works:

  • Thiazide diuretics, often called water pills, help your kidneys remove extra salt and water
  • ACE inhibitors relax your blood vessels by blocking a hormone that narrows them
  • ARBs (angiotensin receptor blockers) work on the same hormone in a different way
  • Calcium channel blockers relax the muscles in your artery walls

For stage 2 hypertension, the guideline now prefers starting with two medicines from different groups combined in a single pill. It brings blood pressure down faster than one medicine, and taking one tablet a day is easier to stick with than two.

Your doctor may adjust the dose or switch medicines over the first few months until your readings reach target. Keep taking your tablets even when your numbers improve, since that improvement generally means the medicine is working, and talk to your doctor before stopping or changing anything.

What Happens If High Blood Pressure Isn't Treated

Untreated high blood pressure slowly damages your blood vessels and the organs they feed, generally over many years and without any warning signs. The extra force wears on the lining of your arteries and makes them stiffer and narrower, which raises your risk of several serious problems.

The American Heart Association's guide to the health threats of high blood pressure lists the main ones:

  • Heart attack, when a damaged artery becomes blocked and cuts off blood flow to part of your heart
  • Stroke, when a blood vessel supplying your brain becomes blocked or bursts
  • Heart failure, when your heart has worked so hard for so long that it enlarges and can't pump enough blood
  • Kidney disease, when damaged arteries around the kidneys stop them filtering your blood well
  • Vision loss, from strained or damaged blood vessels in your eyes
  • Sexual problems, including erectile dysfunction in men and lower sex drive in women
  • Peripheral artery disease, when narrowed arteries in the legs cause pain or tiredness when you walk

The 2025 guideline also recommends keeping blood pressure below 130 to help protect your memory and thinking as you age, since high blood pressure is linked to a higher risk of dementia.

This damage builds slowly, so bringing your blood pressure down lowers your risk, whatever your age and however long your numbers have been high. Every point you take off your reading helps protect your heart, brain and kidneys.

When to Get Emergency Help

A reading of 180/120 or higher is called a hypertensive crisis, and what you should do depends on whether you have symptoms. The American Heart Association's advice on when to call 911 separates the two situations.

If your reading is 180/120 or higher and you have any of these symptoms, call 911 or your local emergency number straight away.

  • Chest pain
  • Shortness of breath
  • Back pain
  • Numbness or weakness
  • Changes in your vision
  • Trouble speaking

Don't wait to see if your blood pressure comes down on its own, and don't drive yourself to hospital. These symptoms can mean the high pressure is already harming your heart, brain or kidneys, and fast treatment can limit the damage.

If your reading is 180/120 or higher and you feel fine, sit quietly for about five minutes and measure again. If the second reading is still that high, contact your doctor the same day, since they may need to adjust or add medication.

This second situation is called hypertensive urgency, and it rarely means a hospital stay. Keep watching for any of the symptoms above while you wait to hear back, and call for emergency help if one appears.

Your One Little Win

One little win

Get one blood pressure reading this week and write it down. Stop at a pharmacy with a free machine, ask at your next clinic visit, or use a validated upper-arm monitor at home. Sit quietly for five minutes first, then note the date, the time and both numbers.

One reading won't diagnose anything, but it gives you a starting point. If it's 130/80 or higher, book a check with your doctor, and if it's normal, you'll have a number to compare against next year.

Frequently Asked Questions About High Blood Pressure

What Is Considered High Blood Pressure?

Under the current US guideline, a reading of 130/80 or higher counts as high blood pressure, also called hypertension. Readings of 120 to 129 for the top number with a bottom number under 80 count as elevated, which is an early warning stage. A diagnosis needs the average of several readings taken on at least two different days.

Does High Blood Pressure Have Symptoms?

Most people with high blood pressure feel completely well, which is why regular checks are the only reliable way to find it. Headaches, nosebleeds, flushing and nervousness aren't reliable signs. Symptoms like chest pain, shortness of breath or vision changes generally appear only when blood pressure is very high, around 180/120 or above, and need emergency care.

What Causes High Blood Pressure in Young Adults?

Most young adults with high blood pressure have the same everyday causes as older adults, including extra weight, salty food, long hours of sitting, alcohol, smoking, poor sleep and family history. A small share have a separate condition driving it, such as kidney disease, a hormone problem or sleep apnea. Your doctor may test for these if your blood pressure is high before 30 without the usual risk factors.

Can High Blood Pressure Be Reversed?

For some people, yes. Lifestyle changes can bring elevated or stage 1 blood pressure back into the normal range, and treating a secondary cause, such as sleep apnea or a medicine, can sometimes bring readings down. Many people need long-term treatment to keep their numbers on target, so if you take tablets, keep taking them and talk to your doctor before making any changes.

When Is High Blood Pressure an Emergency?

A reading of 180/120 or higher with chest pain, shortness of breath, back pain, numbness or weakness, vision changes or trouble speaking is an emergency, so call 911 or your local emergency number. If you get a reading that high but feel fine, wait five minutes and measure again. If it's still that high, contact your doctor the same day.

What Should My Blood Pressure Be?

For most adults, the goal is a reading below 130/80, and a normal reading is below 120/80. Your doctor may set a personal target based on your age and other health conditions, so ask what number you're aiming for at your next check.
