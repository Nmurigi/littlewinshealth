Best Blood Ketone Meters for Diabetics: An Honest Guide to Accuracy, Cost, and When to Test

14 min read · August 2026 · Little Wins Health

A blood ketone meter costs about the same as a blood glucose meter, sometimes less, and a few brands will hand you the device for nothing. The strips are the real expense. And for someone with diabetes, the reading a meter gives can be the difference between riding out a rough day at home and a trip to the emergency room.

Ketones are what your body makes when it burns fat for fuel instead of glucose. A small amount is normal, and it turns up when you fast, exercise hard, or eat low-carb. The problem for someone with diabetes is that ketones also climb when there is not enough insulin to move glucose into your cells, and at that point they stop being harmless and start being a warning.

So this guide does three things. It looks at which blood ketone meters give readings you can trust, what they actually cost to run, and, just as important, when you should be testing at all. Catching a rise at the right moment is how people get ahead of a dangerous situation while it is still manageable.

Some of these meters read both glucose and ketones from one device, so if you have already worked through the blood glucose meters we cover in our companion guide, you may be part of the way there. We will point out which picks pull double duty.

A quick note on terms Blood ketone meter, blood ketone monitor, and ketone testing kit all mean the same thing, a small device that reads the ketones in a drop of blood from a test strip. What it measures is beta-hydroxybutyrate, or BHB, the ketone that shows up first and is the one worth catching early. Urine strips and breath meters measure different things, and we compare all three further down.

Ketosis vs Ketoacidosis (Know the Difference)

These two words look almost the same and get mixed up constantly, but for someone with diabetes the gap between them is enormous. One is a normal state your body can sit in safely. The other is a medical emergency.

Ketosis is the everyday version. When you eat few carbs, fast, or exercise hard, your liver makes ketones for fuel and your blood level rises a little, usually somewhere around 0.5 to 3.0 mmol/L. Your glucose stays normal, your insulin is present and doing its job, and nothing is going wrong. This is the state people on a keto or low-carb diet are after.

Ketoacidosis is the dangerous version, and it comes from a shortage of insulin rather than a shortage of carbs. Without enough insulin, glucose cannot get into your cells, so your body burns fat harder and harder and floods the blood with ketones while your blood sugar climbs at the same time.

The blood turns acidic, and diabetic ketoacidosis, or DKA, can become life-threatening within hours. It is most common in type 1 diabetes, but it happens in type 2 as well, especially during illness or for people who use insulin.

Here is the part a meter alone cannot tell you. The same reading of 1.5 mmol/L means two very different things depending on who you are.

For a low-carb dieter with normal blood sugar, it is simply deep nutritional ketosis. For someone with type 1 diabetes and a high glucose reading, it is a signal to act. The number counts, but the context around it counts for more.

That difference comes down to insulin. If you want the fuller picture of what happens inside the body when insulin runs short, we cover it separately, but the short version is this. Ketosis is your body using a backup fuel while everything else still works, and ketoacidosis is that backup running out of control because the system that keeps it in check has gone missing.

If you have diabetes and your ketones are rising alongside a high blood sugar, that is not something to manage with diet, it is a time to follow your care team's plan and get help. We cover exactly when to test, and what each level means, in the sections just below.

How Ketone Testing Works: Blood, Urine, and Breath

Your body sends ketones to three places, and each kind of home test reads a different one. That sounds like a small detail, but for someone with diabetes it decides which test you can trust when it counts.

A blood ketone meter reads beta-hydroxybutyrate, the BHB from earlier. This is the ketone that rises first and makes up most of what your body produces when ketones are climbing toward trouble.

From a small drop of blood you get a number in mmol/L within seconds, and it reflects what is happening right now. That is why hospitals and diabetes guidelines treat blood BHB as the standard for spotting and tracking ketoacidosis, and why a blood meter is the one to reach for if DKA is a risk for you.

Urine strips read a different ketone, acetoacetate, and they work by changing color on a dipstick. They are cheap and need no blood, which is their appeal. But the limits are real.

Urine shows ketones your body cleared a while ago rather than your level this minute, so it runs behind. How much you have had to drink shifts the result. And early in a dangerous rise, when BHB is doing the climbing, a urine strip can still read low, because it cannot see BHB at all.

In a systematic review of type 1 diabetes, people who tested blood rather than urine reached the emergency room less often and recovered from DKA faster.

There is one more quirk worth knowing. As DKA is treated and starts to clear, BHB converts into acetoacetate, so a urine strip can read worse just as you are getting better. That backwards signal is a big part of why blood testing took over.

Why blood beats urine for DKA Urine dipsticks cannot detect beta-hydroxybutyrate, the very ketone that leads the way into diabetic ketoacidosis. A blood meter can. That is the whole reason blood testing is now the standard for anyone with diabetes who is at risk of DKA.

Breath meters are the third option. They read acetone, another form ketones take, and they need no strips and no blood, which makes them reusable and popular with people following a keto diet. For tracking nutritional ketosis they are convenient. But they have not been proven for catching ketoacidosis, so for diabetes a breath meter is a wellness gadget rather than a safety net.

So the ranking for diabetes is not close. A blood ketone meter catches trouble early and tracks it honestly. Urine strips are a fallback. Breath meters are made for a different job.

The rest of this guide is about blood meters, because for managing the risk that comes with diabetes, that is the test that earns its place.

When to Test for Ketones

Testing ketones is only useful if you do it at the right moments, and for most people those moments are predictable. You do not need to test every day. You test when the odds of a problem go up, and the situations that raise those odds are worth knowing by heart.

Reach for your meter in any of these situations:

  • When you are ill: Any cold, flu, infection, or stomach bug raises your risk, because illness makes your body need more insulin. Test whenever you are unwell, even if your blood sugar looks normal.
  • When your blood sugar is high: A reading at or above 240 mg/dL (13.3 mmol/L), especially one that stays there, is a common trigger to check ketones.
  • When you have DKA symptoms: Nausea, vomiting, stomach pain, a fruity smell on your breath, deep or rapid breathing, unusual thirst, or confusion all warrant a test straight away.
  • When insulin has been missed: A skipped dose, a failed pump, or a kinked pump line lets ketones build, so test if your insulin has not gone in as planned.
  • Before hard exercise with high glucose: If your blood sugar is above 240 mg/dL (13.3 mmol/L) before a workout, check ketones first, and skip the session if they are raised.

Once you start, keep going. If you are ill or your blood sugar stays high, the CDC and the American Diabetes Association advise checking every 4 to 6 hours, day and night, until things settle. One reading is a snapshot. A run of them tells you which way the trend is heading, which is what actually guides your next move.

A special warning for SGLT2 medicines If you take an SGLT2 inhibitor such as Jardiance, Farxiga, Invokana, or Steglatro, you can develop DKA even when your blood sugar is normal or only a little high. This is called euglycemic DKA, and it means the glucose number alone will not warn you. Test ketones whenever you feel unwell, whatever your blood sugar reads. Many care teams advise pausing these medicines during illness, so ask yours what to do before you ever need to.

Not everyone carries the same risk. DKA is most associated with type 1 diabetes, where ketone testing during any illness is a fixed rule, but it reaches people with type 2 as well, particularly those who use insulin or take an SGLT2 medicine. If you are not sure where you sit, our guide to how type 1 and type 2 diabetes differ lays out who tends to be most exposed and why.

Testing is your early warning system. It flags trouble, but it cannot treat it, so what you do with a rising reading is what counts. If your ketones are climbing rather than falling, if you cannot keep fluids down, or if you have deep breathing, chest pain, or confusion, that is the point to stop managing it at home and get urgent care.

A blood ketone reading above 3.0 mmol/L is a medical emergency at any time, and when in doubt you should call your care team or emergency services, because DKA moves fast and is far easier to treat early.

Blood Ketone Levels Chart (What Your Reading Means)

Once you have a number, you need to know what to do with it. Blood ketones are read in mmol/L, and the ranges below follow clinical guidance on what each level means. Treat them as a guide rather than a replacement for the plan your care team has given you.

What Your Blood Ketone Reading Means
ReadingWhat It MeansWhat to Do
Under 0.6 mmol/LNormal.Nothing special. Carry on as usual.
0.6 to 1.5 mmol/LSlightly raised.Drink water, follow your insulin plan, and recheck in about two hours.
1.6 to 2.9 mmol/LHigh, with a real risk of DKA.Contact your care team now for advice.

If you use urine strips instead, the result comes as a color scale from negative up to large, not a number. A reading of moderate or large, often shown as two plus signs or more, lines up with the high end of the chart and is a signal to call for help. Remember that urine lags behind blood, so a blood meter gives you an earlier and clearer picture.

What to Look For in a Blood Ketone Meter

Once you get past the packaging, only a few things separate a ketone meter worth owning from one that will let you down. Here is what actually earns its place:

  • Accuracy you can check: Look for a meter that is FDA cleared or CE marked and reads beta-hydroxybutyrate, since that is the ketone that warns you first. Independent accuracy data is thinner for ketone meters than for glucose meters, so a clear regulatory clearance carries more weight here.
  • Dual glucose and ketone testing: A meter that reads both from one device saves you carrying two, and it lets you see ketones and blood sugar together, which is the pair that tells you how much to worry.
  • Strip cost and supply: Ketone strips are the real expense and the range is wide, so check the price per strip and that you can reorder them easily before you commit to a system.
  • Simple, quick testing: No coding, a small blood sample, and a result in about ten seconds are now standard, and they help a lot on a day you are ill and need an answer fast.
  • A memory or an app: Stored readings, or an app that logs them, let you and your care team see the trend rather than one lonely number, which is what guides the next step.

Best for Accuracy and Clinical Trust: Precision Xtra

Best for people who want the ketone meter that hospitals and researchers lean on. Precision Xtra from Abbott reads both glucose and ketones from one device, returns a result in ten seconds, and is the meter often used as the reference in accuracy studies.

What stands out is its track record. It has been the standard blood ketone system for years, which is why clinicians trust it. The catch is the cost, since its ketone strips run around five to six dollars each, far more than any other meter here, which makes it the most expensive to live with.

Best for Both Glucose and Ketones With an App: Keto-Mojo GK+

Best for people who want one meter for glucose and ketones, plus an app that does the tracking. The Keto-Mojo GK+ is FDA cleared, syncs over Bluetooth to a free app, and works out the glucose ketone index for you.

What stands out is the mix of price and features. Its ketone strips cost about a dollar each, among the cheapest here, and the company says it has featured in over a hundred clinical trials, though that figure comes from its own materials rather than independent testing. For most people weighing accuracy, cost, and ease, it is the easiest meter to recommend.

Best for Lower-Cost Strips From a Medical Meter: Nova Max Plus

Best for people who want a plain, medical-style meter with ketone strips that cost less than the Precision Xtra system. The Nova Max Plus reads glucose and ketones, needs only a tiny drop of blood, and switches to ketone mode on its own when you insert a ketone strip.

What stands out is that it pairs a familiar, no-frills design with more affordable ketone strips. It is also the meter that bridges from our blood glucose guide, since the same device many people already use for glucose can add ketone testing. There is no app, so you log readings yourself, which suits anyone who prefers to keep things simple.

Best for Tracking More Than Ketones: FORA 6 Connect

Best for data-minded people who want one device that measures several things, not only glucose and ketones. The FORA 6 Connect is a multi-parameter meter with a Bluetooth app that also works out the glucose ketone index and the Dr. Boz ratio.

What stands out is its range of measurements, which appeals if you like detailed tracking. The honest caveat is that the device is not clinically validated in the way the others here are, and its strips can cost more, so it is a niche pick rather than a first choice for diabetes safety.

Best for an Accurate Meter on a Budget: KetoSens

Best for people who want reliable blood ketone readings without paying a premium. KetoSens is FDA cleared, pairs with an app, and in hands-on reviews reads closely in line with pricier meters like the Keto-Mojo.

What stands out is the value. It focuses on ketones rather than trying to do everything, and it keeps both the meter and the strips affordable, which makes it a sensible starting point if you are new to ketone testing.

What It Will Really Cost You

Like glucose meters, the device is the cheap part and the strips are the bill you keep paying. But blood ketone strips cost far more than glucose strips, so the sums are worth doing before you pick a system.

Most blood ketone strips land between about 80 cents and $1.50 each, with the Keto-Mojo near a dollar and the Precision Xtra the outlier at roughly five to six dollars. How much that adds up to depends entirely on how often you test.

Take someone who tests twice a day through a stable spell. At a dollar a strip that runs about $60 a month, or a little over $700 a year. Choose Precision Xtra strips at five to six dollars each and the same routine climbs past $300 a month. Same testing, a very different bill.

This is where cheap urine strips have their appeal. A tub of them costs only a few dollars and lasts a long time, so keeping some as a backup is sensible. Just remember what you give up, because urine lags behind blood and can miss an early rise, which makes it a budget backup rather than a substitute for accuracy when it counts.

One little win

Before you buy a meter, price a box of its ketone strips and work out what a bad sick week would cost, testing four times a day for three days. That figure, not the meter's price, tells you what the system really costs on the day you need it most.

A Note on Keto and Nutritional Ketosis

Plenty of people buy these meters for a keto or low-carb diet rather than for diabetes, and the same device serves both. The difference is what the numbers mean for you.

In nutritional ketosis, blood ketones usually sit between 0.5 and 3.0 mmol/L, and a dieter is often trying to stay in that band to burn fat for fuel. Some track the glucose ketone index, or GKI, which divides glucose by ketones to gauge how deep the ketosis is, and meters like the Keto-Mojo work it out automatically.

Here is the line that keeps this safe. For someone without diabetes, a reading of 2.0 mmol/L is a diet going to plan. For someone on insulin, the same reading alongside high blood sugar is a warning. The number is identical, the meaning is not, so read your ketones against your own situation and never against a keto chart made for someone else.

Paying Less: Insurance and the NHS

Ketone strips can be a real running cost, so it helps to know what your coverage will and will not pay for, because it is often less than you would expect.

If you rely on insurance, there is a catch worth knowing. Blood glucose strips are usually covered as durable medical equipment, but Medicare does not treat ketone strips the same way, and many private plans follow suit. Some plans fold them into an over-the-counter allowance, and an HSA or FSA will usually reimburse them, especially with a prescription, so ask your plan and your doctor before paying full price.

Under the NHS, blood ketone testing is provided for people who need it, mainly those with type 1 or anyone on insulin, as part of preventing DKA. The meter is usually supplied through your clinic, and the strips come on prescription. Those prescriptions are free if your diabetes is treated with medication, through a medical exemption certificate in England and for everyone in Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland.

Final Thoughts

If one idea should stick, it is this. A blood ketone meter is not something you use every day, but on the days you do need it, it can be the difference between a manageable scare and a hospital stay. That is worth getting right.

Buy a meter that measures beta-hydroxybutyrate, is cleared by a regulator, and uses strips you can afford to keep on hand. Learn the readings that mean act now and the ones that mean call for help. And keep it, along with fresh strips, somewhere you can find it on a day you are too unwell to go hunting.

Ketones are one more signal your body gives you, and a good meter simply lets you hear it early. For the wider picture of managing the condition day to day, our diabetes guides pull the rest together. Get the reading you can trust, act on it, and let the small, early catches add up to something safer.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a dangerous ketone level?

For blood ketones, anything from 1.6 to 2.9 mmol/L signals a real risk of DKA and a call to your care team, and 3.0 mmol/L or above is a medical emergency. Below 0.6 mmol/L is normal, and 0.6 to 1.5 mmol/L is mildly raised, worth more fluids and a recheck. These are general guides, so follow the plan your care team has set for you.

Are blood or urine ketone strips better?

Blood strips are better for anyone managing diabetes. They measure beta-hydroxybutyrate, the ketone that rises first, and give a real-time number, while urine strips measure a different ketone that lags behind and can miss an early rise. Urine strips are cheaper and fine as a backup, but blood is the one to trust when it counts.

Can people with type 2 diabetes get DKA?

Yes. DKA is most common in type 1, but people with type 2 can develop it too, especially during illness, if they use insulin, or if they take an SGLT2 medicine, which can trigger DKA even at normal blood sugar. If you fall into any of those groups, ketone testing during illness is worth taking seriously.

Do I need a prescription to buy a ketone meter?

No. Blood ketone meters and strips are sold over the counter, in pharmacies and online, without a prescription. A prescription mainly helps with insurance coverage or HSA reimbursement rather than with buying the meter itself.

Can one meter test both glucose and ketones?

Yes, and several do. The Precision Xtra, Nova Max Plus, Keto-Mojo GK+, and FORA 6 all read glucose and ketones from the same device, using a separate strip for each. If you already test glucose, a dual meter lets you add ketone testing without carrying a second gadget.

How often should I replace a ketone meter?

Most last for years, so there is no set schedule. Replace one if you drop it hard, if control-solution checks fail, or if its strips are discontinued. Otherwise, fresh, in-date strips count for more than the age of the meter.
