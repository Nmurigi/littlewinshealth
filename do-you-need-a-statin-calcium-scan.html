Do You Need a Statin? How a Heart Calcium Scan Can Help You Decide

9 min read · November 2026 · By Anne M.

Your doctor runs your numbers and says it might be time to start a statin. You feel fine, you've never had heart trouble, and you're not sure you want to take a pill every day for the rest of your life.

A heart calcium scan can help you and your doctor decide. It shows if hard plaque has already collected in the arteries that feed your heart. In a large 2026 trial, people whose statin advice came from this scan were advised to take statins far less often, and they had the same rate of heart attacks and strokes over about 4 years.

A second 2026 trial looked at the other end of the decision, stopping statins after 70. Neither trial applies if you've already had a heart attack or stroke. For you, statins are a well-proven way to lower the risk of another one.

The Short Answer

  • In the CorCal Outcomes trial of 5,772 adults, the group whose advice came from a risk calculator was told to take a statin more than three times as often as the group whose advice came from a calcium scan.
  • After about 4 years, 2.7% of people in each group had a heart attack, stroke or artery procedure, or died.
  • Fewer heart events happened than expected, so the trial couldn't prove the scan works as well as the calculator.
  • In the STREAM trial, adults 70 and over without heart disease who stopped their statin did about as well at 2 years. They had slightly more heart events by 4 years.

What the 2026 Calcium Scan Statin Trial Found

The CorCal Outcomes trial found that when a calcium scan guided the statin decision, far fewer people were advised to take one, and heart attacks, strokes and deaths stayed at the same rate. Researchers presented the results at the European Society of Cardiology Congress in Munich on 31 August 2026.

The trial included 5,772 adults treated through Intermountain Health in the western US. Their average age was 64, and 51% were women. None had heart disease or diabetes, and none had taken a statin before.

Half the group got statin advice from a standard risk calculator. A calculator estimates your chance of a heart attack or stroke from details like your age, blood pressure, cholesterol and smoking. The other half got advice based on their calcium score, and in both groups patients and their doctors made the final call.

The risk calculator group was advised to take a statin more than three times as often as the calcium scan group. Even so, after an average of 4.2 years, 2.7% of people in each group had a heart attack, stroke or artery procedure such as a stent, or died.

People in the scan group were also much more likely to take the statin they were advised to take. Among those advised by their scan, 62% stuck with it, compared with 23% of those advised by the calculator. The researchers didn't explain why, though seeing proof of your own plaque may make the advice easier to act on.

The trial has one big limit. Fewer people had heart events than the researchers expected, so the study couldn't prove the scan approach works as well as the calculator. The lead researcher, Dr. Joseph Muhlestein of Intermountain Medical Center, said the results could help plan a larger trial comparing the two methods.

For now, a calcium score works best as one more piece of information that you and your doctor weigh along with your other risk factors.

What Your Coronary Artery Calcium Score Means

A coronary artery calcium score shows how much hardened plaque sits in the arteries that supply your heart. A score of zero means none was found, and the higher the number, the more plaque there is.

The test is a quick CT scan of your chest, with no needles or dye. It takes 10 to 15 minutes and uses about 1 millisievert of radiation. Scores start at zero and can go above 1,000, as Harvard Health explains in its guide to calcium scores.

Your report generally gives two numbers. The first is your score. The second is a percentile that compares you with people of your age and sex. A 75th percentile result means you have more plaque than 75 out of 100 people like you.

Any score above zero means some plaque has already formed in your heart arteries. The 2018 US cholesterol guideline uses the score when your statin decision isn't clear-cut, roughly like this.

What your calcium score generally means for a statin
Calcium scoreWhat it generally means
0A statin can generally wait, with another scan in 5 to 10 years
1 to 99Some plaque, and a statin is generally favored, especially from age 55
100 or more, or 75th percentile or higherA statin is generally recommended

A zero score doesn't always mean you can skip a statin. The guidelines make exceptions for people who smoke, have diabetes or have a strong family history of early heart disease. The scan also only picks up calcified plaque, so softer plaque that hasn't hardened yet can go unseen.

Who Should Consider a Calcium Score Test

A calcium score helps most when your heart risk falls in the middle, where the case for a statin could go either way. If your risk is clearly low or clearly high, the scan is unlikely to change your doctor's advice.

A study published in JAMA in August 2026 followed more than 6,000 adults aged 45 to 79 for 10 years. For the group as a whole, adding a calcium score to PREVENT, the American Heart Association's risk calculator, improved its predictions only slightly. For people whose 10-year risk was 3% to 9%, the score gave a much clearer picture of who was more or less likely to have a heart attack or stroke.

US cholesterol guidelines say a calcium score may be worth considering if you

  • are between 40 and 75
  • don't have diabetes
  • have LDL cholesterol between 70 and 189 mg/dL (1.8 to 4.9 mmol/L)
  • and you and your doctor still aren't sure about a statin after going over your risk

The scan is less useful if you already have heart disease, since you'd be treated anyway. It's also generally not recommended under 40, because plaque in younger people often hasn't hardened enough to show up. If your risk is high, guidelines advise a statin whatever the scan shows, so the test adds little.

The scan is quick, relatively cheap and getting easier to find. Even so, the JAMA study's senior author, Dr. Nilay Shah of Northwestern University, warned that scanning people at low risk can lead to unneeded radiation, extra tests and costs with no clear benefit. Talk it through with your doctor before booking one.

Calcium Scans vs Risk Calculators for Statin Decisions

A risk calculator estimates your chance of a heart attack or stroke from your health numbers. A calcium scan looks directly at your own arteries for plaque that has already formed.

The calculator uses details like your age, sex, blood pressure, cholesterol, and whether you smoke or have diabetes. It's quick and comes as part of a routine checkup. Because it works from averages for people like you, though, two people with the same numbers get the same result, even if only one of them has plaque.

A calcium scan fills in that missing piece. In the CorCal Outcomes trial, advice based on the scan pointed far fewer people toward a statin, and heart events stayed the same in both groups. People who were advised by their scan were also much more likely to take their statin, at 62% compared with 23%.

Doctors generally use the two together, starting with the calculator and adding a scan only if the statin decision is still unclear. US and European cholesterol guidelines both treat the score as extra information for people near the treatment threshold. Blood pressure feeds straight into your risk number too, so small daily habits that lower blood pressure can bring your overall heart risk down.

Can You Stop Taking Statins After 70? What the STREAM Trial Found

For adults 70 and over who've never had heart disease, stopping a statin carried little extra risk over 2 years, though the risk crept up over longer follow-up. Researchers presented the STREAM trial at the American Heart Association's Scientific Sessions on 7 November 2026, and it was published the same day in the New England Journal of Medicine.

The trial included 1,880 adults with an average age of 77, at 24 clinical centers in Switzerland, France and the Netherlands. Everyone was taking a statin to prevent heart disease, and none had had a heart attack, stroke or other heart disease. Each person also lived with at least two other long-term health conditions.

About half stopped their statin, and the other half kept taking it. At about 2 years, the group that stopped had no significant rise in heart attacks, strokes or deaths. That worked out to roughly 1 extra heart event for every 40 people who stopped.

Many people hope that stopping will ease side effects, but that didn't show up in the results. The two groups had the same rates of muscle pain, falls and muscle loss, and the same quality of life. A separate 2026 trial in adults 75 and over, called SAGA/SITE, also found no quality of life benefit from stopping.

Over up to 4 years of follow-up, 12.7% of people who stopped their statin had a heart attack, stroke or died, compared with 9.2% of those who kept taking it. Dr. Rita Redberg of the University of California, San Francisco, quoted in the American Heart Association's release, said this small increase in risk means there can't be one general rule for everyone. She said the choice should be a shared decision between you and your doctor.

If you've had a heart attack, stroke or other heart disease, these findings don't apply to you, and you shouldn't stop a statin without talking to your doctor first.

Questions to Ask Your Doctor About Statins and a Calcium Score

A short list of questions can turn a rushed appointment into a real conversation about your heart. Write these down, or save them on your phone, and bring them to your next visit.

  1. What's my 10-year risk of a heart attack or stroke? Ask for the actual number, since it decides if a calcium scan is likely to help.
  2. Would a calcium score change your advice for me? If your risk is clearly high or clearly low, the answer may be no, which saves you a scan you don't need.
  3. What score would make you recommend a statin? Knowing this in advance helps you understand your result when it comes back.
  4. Do I have anything that means I need a statin even with a zero score? Smoking, diabetes and a strong family history of early heart disease can all count.
  5. If I'm already on a statin and over 70, what would happen if I stopped? Ask how the STREAM results apply to your health conditions and the other medicines you take.
  6. When should we look at this decision again? A zero score is generally rechecked in 5 to 10 years, and a decision to stop a statin is worth reviewing too.

If high blood pressure is part of your heart risk, it helps to know what your blood pressure numbers mean and how they're treated before the appointment too.

Key Takeaway

A heart calcium scan can show if plaque has already formed in your arteries, and in a 2026 trial it pointed far fewer people toward a statin with no rise in heart events. It works best when your risk is in the middle and the statin decision is unclear. If you're 70 or over and have never had heart disease, stopping a statin may be an option, but the risk rises slightly over time, so make that choice with your doctor.

Frequently Asked Questions About Statins and Calcium Scores

Can a Calcium Score of Zero Mean I Don't Need a Statin?

Often, yes. US guidelines say a statin can generally be held off with a zero score and the scan repeated in 5 to 10 years. The exceptions are people who smoke, have diabetes or have a strong family history of early heart disease.

Is a Calcium Scan Safe?

A calcium scan is a quick CT scan with no needles or dye, and it uses about 1 millisievert of radiation. The radiation is low, but it isn't zero. That's one reason doctors don't recommend the scan for people at low risk.

How Much Does a Calcium Score Test Cost?

Prices depend on where you live and the clinic you use. The scan is generally seen as relatively inexpensive, and it's becoming cheaper and easier to find. Ask the clinic for the price before you book, and check if your insurance covers it.

Should I Stop My Statin If I'm Over 70?

Not on your own. In the STREAM trial, adults 70 and over without heart disease who stopped had similar results at 2 years, but slightly more heart attacks, strokes and deaths over up to 4 years. Stopping is a shared decision to make with your doctor, and it doesn't apply if you've had a heart attack or stroke.

Can a Calcium Score Go Down on Statins?

Generally, no. Statins can shrink the softer, riskier part of plaque while turning some of it into dense calcium, which makes the plaque more stable. So your score may stay the same or even rise while your risk goes down, and doctors generally don't use repeat scans to check if a statin is working.
