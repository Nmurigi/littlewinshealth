Type 1 vs. Type 2 Diabetes: What's the Difference?

Your doctor says you have type 2 diabetes, and your mind goes straight to your coworker's son, who has taken insulin since he was eight. Both conditions are called diabetes, yet his days of injections and finger pricks look very different from the tablets and food advice you've just been given.

Type 1 and type 2 diabetes both leave too much sugar in your blood, but they get there in different ways. In type 1, your body stops making insulin, the hormone that moves sugar out of your blood and into your cells. In type 2, your body still makes insulin but can't use it well.

This guide compares type 1 vs type 2 diabetes side by side, covering what causes each one, how the symptoms and tests differ and how each type is treated.

The Short Answer

  • Both types cause high blood sugar, but for different reasons.
  • In type 1 diabetes, the immune system destroys the cells that make insulin, so the body makes little or none.
  • In type 2 diabetes, the body makes insulin, but the cells don't respond to it properly. Doctors call this insulin resistance.
  • Type 2 is far more common, at about 90% to 95% of cases, while type 1 accounts for about 5% to 10%.
  • Type 1 generally appears quickly and often starts in childhood, while type 2 builds slowly over years and is most common after 45. Either type can start at any age.
  • Everyone with type 1 needs insulin every day. Type 2 is managed with lifestyle changes, tablets and sometimes insulin.
  • Neither type is your fault.

Type 2 is by far the more common type. More than 40 million Americans have diabetes, and about 90% to 95% of them have type 2, while about 5% to 10% of people with diabetes have type 1.

The table shows what's typical, and real life has exceptions. Adults can develop type 1, and a growing number of children, teens and young adults now develop type 2, so age alone can't tell you which type someone has.

What Is the Difference Between Type 1 and Type 2 Diabetes?

The difference comes down to insulin. In type 1 diabetes, your body can't make insulin, and in type 2 diabetes, your body makes insulin but struggles to use it.

Insulin is a hormone made in your pancreas, and the way insulin moves sugar out of your blood works like a key in a lock. When you eat, sugar from your food enters your blood, and insulin opens your cells so that sugar can move inside and be used for energy.

In type 1, the key is missing. The cells that make insulin have been destroyed, so sugar stays in the blood because nothing can let it into the cells.

In type 2, the key is there but the lock is stiff. Your cells stop responding to insulin the way they should, and although your pancreas makes extra to force the door open, it can't keep up forever.

What Causes Type 1 Diabetes?

Type 1 diabetes is an autoimmune disease, which means the immune system attacks part of the body by mistake. In this case, it destroys the cells in the pancreas that make insulin, called beta cells, and this attack can go on quietly for months or years before any symptoms appear.

Researchers don't yet know exactly why the immune system does this, but two factors seem to work together:

  • Genes, since having a parent, brother or sister with type 1 raises your chance of developing it
  • A trigger in the environment, such as a virus, that may set off the immune reaction in people whose genes put them at risk

Diet and lifestyle habits don't cause type 1 diabetes. If your child has just been diagnosed, nothing you fed them and nothing they did brought it on, and there was no way you could have prevented it.

What Causes Type 2 Diabetes?

Type 2 diabetes develops when your body has trouble using insulin and your pancreas can't make enough to make up for it. The process starts with insulin resistance, which is when the cells in your muscles, fat and liver don't respond well to insulin.

At first, your pancreas copes by making more insulin, and your blood sugar stays in the normal range. Over the years, it falls behind and blood sugar starts to rise. The stage where it's higher than normal but below the diabetes range is called prediabetes, and a prediabetes diagnosis is often the first sign that insulin resistance has been building.

Researchers don't fully understand what causes insulin resistance, but you're more likely to develop type 2 diabetes if you:

  • Have a family history of type 2 diabetes
  • Are 45 or older, although more young people are now developing it
  • Carry extra weight, especially around your waist
  • Aren't physically active
  • Are African American, American Indian, Asian American, Hispanic, Latino or Pacific Islander

Weight and activity get most of the attention, but some risk factors, such as your age, family history and race, are outside your control. That's why a diagnosis is no reason for blame or shame.

Type 1 vs. Type 2 Diabetes Symptoms

Type 1 and type 2 diabetes share the same main symptoms, and the biggest difference is how fast they appear. According to the CDC's list of diabetes symptoms, both types can cause:

  • Urinating often
  • Feeling very thirsty or hungry
  • Losing weight without trying
  • Feeling very tired
  • Blurry vision
  • Irritability or mood changes
  • Frequent urinary tract or yeast infections

How Type 1 Symptoms Appear

Type 1 symptoms can come on suddenly, over a few weeks or months, and they can be severe. People with type 1 may also have nausea, vomiting or stomach pain.

In children, the warning signs often include bed-wetting, increased thirst, tiredness and weight loss. These signs need immediate attention, so contact your child's doctor the same day if you notice them together.

How Type 2 Symptoms Appear

Type 2 symptoms often take several years to develop, and many people have none at all. Some find out from a routine blood test, and others only when they develop signs of another health problem caused by diabetes, such as numbness or tingling in the feet or hands, or changes in their vision.

Because the symptoms are so easy to miss, knowing your risk factors and getting tested is more reliable than waiting to feel unwell.

Diabetic Ketoacidosis, an Emergency Sign

When the body has too little insulin, it burns fat for fuel and produces acids called ketones. If ketones build up too fast, they cause diabetic ketoacidosis, or DKA, which is a medical emergency.

DKA is most common in people with type 1 diabetes, although people with type 2 can develop it too.

If you have diabetes and you're sick, or your blood sugar is 250 mg/dL (13.9 mmol/L) or above, checking for ketones at home can catch DKA early. Go to the emergency room or call 911 if:

  • Your blood sugar stays at 300 mg/dL (16.7 mmol/L) or above
  • Your breath smells fruity
  • You're vomiting and can't keep food or drinks down
  • You're having trouble breathing
  • You have several DKA symptoms at once, such as extreme thirst, fast deep breathing, stomach pain and extreme tiredness

How Each Type Is Diagnosed

Doctors diagnose type 1 and type 2 diabetes with the same blood tests. The NIDDK's guide to diabetes tests lists the results that point to diabetes:

  • A1C test, 6.5% or above
  • Fasting blood sugar test, 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) or above
  • Oral glucose tolerance test, 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) or above two hours after a sugary drink
  • Random blood sugar test, 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) or above in someone who has symptoms

The A1C test shows your average blood sugar over the past three months, and a normal A1C is below 5.7%. Your doctor will generally repeat a test on another day to confirm the result before making a diagnosis.

How Doctors Tell Type 1 From Type 2

These tests show that you have diabetes, but they can't show which type. Doctors work that out from your age, your weight, your family history and how quickly your symptoms appeared.

When the picture is unclear, a blood test for autoantibodies can help. Autoantibodies are immune proteins that attack the body's own cells by mistake, and finding them in your blood suggests type 1 diabetes.

LADA, the Type 1 Diabetes That Looks Like Type 2

Some adults diagnosed with type 2 diabetes actually have a slow form of type 1 called latent autoimmune diabetes in adults, or LADA. You may also hear it called type 1.5 diabetes.

In LADA, the immune system attacks the insulin-making cells, as it does in type 1, but the damage happens slowly and generally starts after age 30. People still make enough insulin at first to manage without injections, so the condition is easy to mistake for type 2.

It's more common than most people expect. An international expert panel found that LADA accounts for 2% to 12% of adults with diabetes, depending on the population studied.

Compared with people who have type 2, adults with LADA are more likely to:

  • Be younger when they're diagnosed
  • Be leaner, with less insulin resistance
  • Have lower blood pressure
  • Have another autoimmune condition, or a close relative who does

How LADA Is Diagnosed

Two blood tests can separate LADA from type 2. The first looks for autoantibodies, and about 90% of people with LADA who have autoantibodies test positive for one called GAD.

The second is a C-peptide test, which shows how much insulin your pancreas still makes. Doctors use the result to choose treatment and repeat it over time, since insulin production can keep falling in LADA.

Why the Right Diagnosis Changes Treatment

Some common type 2 tablets, called sulfonylureas, aren't recommended for LADA, and doctors watch insulin production closely so that insulin can be started when it's needed. The expert panel found insulin to be effective and safe for people with LADA.

If you were diagnosed with type 2 as a younger, leaner adult, or you have another autoimmune condition, it's reasonable to ask your doctor about an autoantibody test. A changed diagnosis means your diabetes was autoimmune from the start, since type 2 diabetes doesn't turn into type 1.

Type 1 vs. Type 2 Diabetes Treatment

Both types are treated with the same goal, which is keeping blood sugar in a healthy range to prevent serious health problems such as heart disease, vision loss and kidney disease. Type 1 always needs insulin, while type 2 has a wider set of options.

Treating Type 1 Diabetes

If you have type 1, you must take insulin every day because your pancreas doesn't make it. Insulin can't be taken as a pill, so it's delivered in one of these ways:

  • Injections, with a needle and syringe or an insulin pen
  • An insulin pump, a small machine that gives regular doses of insulin through the day by way of a thin tube under the skin
  • An automated insulin delivery system, sometimes called an artificial pancreas, which links a continuous glucose monitor to a pump and adjusts your insulin automatically

You'll also check your blood sugar regularly, and your doctor will help you work out the right type and dose of insulin. Healthy eating and exercise help keep blood sugar in range, but they can't replace insulin.

Treating Type 2 Diabetes

Treatment for type 2 generally builds in stages, and many people use more than one approach:

  • Healthy eating and regular activity, which can begin with walking for 10 minutes after dinner and filling your grocery cart with foods that are kinder to blood sugar
  • Metformin, the tablet most people with type 2 start with
  • Other medicines, added over time if blood sugar stays high, including GLP-1 injections, which also reduce hunger and help with weight loss
  • Insulin, which some people need long term and others need only for a period, such as during pregnancy or a hospital stay
  • Weight-loss surgery, an option for some people who have obesity and type 2 diabetes

Some people with type 2 keep their blood sugar in range through lifestyle changes alone. If you need tablets or insulin later, it means your body needs more support, and that's a normal part of treatment for many people.

Can You Prevent or Reverse Either Type?

The answer is different for each type. Type 1 can't be prevented or reversed today, while type 2 can often be prevented or delayed, and some people can put it into remission.

Type 1 Diabetes

Nobody knows how to prevent type 1 diabetes yet, and once it develops, insulin treatment is lifelong. What has changed is how early it can be found.

A blood test for autoantibodies can spot type 1 before symptoms begin, and doctors may suggest it if you have a parent, brother or sister with the condition. For people whose results show a high risk, a medicine called teplizumab can delay the start of symptoms and the need for insulin.

Type 2 Diabetes

Type 2 diabetes can often be prevented or delayed. In the Diabetes Prevention Program, a large US study of adults at high risk, lifestyle changes lowered the chance of developing type 2 diabetes by 58% over about three years. Participants aimed to lose 7% of their body weight and be active for 150 minutes a week, and the benefit was even larger, at 71%, for adults aged 60 and older.

Some people who already have type 2 can bring their blood sugar back below the diabetes range without medication, which doctors call remission. In the UK's DiRECT trial, which tested a structured weight management program run through family doctors, 46% of participants were in remission after one year and 36% after two years.

Weight loss had the biggest effect in that trial. Among participants who lost more than 10 kg (22 lb), 64% were in remission at two years.

Remission means your A1C stays below 6.5% without diabetes medication, but high blood sugar can return, so regular checks continue. The trial's program was medically supervised, so talk to your doctor before trying a very low-calorie diet, especially if you take tablets or insulin.

Your One Little Win

One little win

At your next appointment, ask your doctor which type of diabetes you have and how they know. It's a simple question, and the answer tells you a lot. You'll learn which tests were used, whether an autoantibody test would be useful, and why your treatment plan looks the way it does.

Write the answer down next to your latest A1C result so you have both in one place.

Frequently Asked Questions About Type 1 and Type 2 Diabetes

Which Is Worse, Type 1 or Type 2 Diabetes?

Both are serious, and both can be treated effectively. Type 1 needs insulin from the first day and can become an emergency quickly, while type 2 is often found late, after high blood sugar has had years to cause damage. With either type, keeping blood sugar close to your target helps prevent or delay complications.

Can Type 2 Diabetes Turn Into Type 1?

No. The two types have different causes, so one can't become the other. Some adults diagnosed with type 2 later learn they have LADA, a slow form of type 1 that was autoimmune from the start. Starting insulin doesn't change your type either, since many people with type 2 need insulin over time.

Can You Get Type 1 Diabetes as an Adult?

Yes. Type 1 is most often diagnosed in children, teens and young adults, but it can start at any age. In some adults it develops slowly, which is why it can be mistaken for type 2 at first.

Is Type 1 or Type 2 Diabetes Genetic?

Family history plays a part in both. Having a parent, brother or sister with type 1 raises your chance of developing it, and a family history of type 2 raises your risk of type 2. Genes alone don't decide the outcome, since a trigger in the environment is also involved in type 1, and weight, activity and age add to the risk of type 2.

Can Type 2 Diabetes Be Reversed?

Some people can put type 2 diabetes into remission, which means blood sugar stays below the diabetes range without medication. In one UK trial of a supervised weight management program, 46% of participants were in remission after one year. Remission was most likely in people who lost the most weight, and blood sugar can rise again, so regular checks are still needed.

Do People With Type 2 Diabetes Need Insulin?

Some do. Many people manage type 2 with lifestyle changes and tablets, but others need insulin over time, or for a short period such as during pregnancy or a hospital stay. Needing insulin is a normal part of treatment for many people with type 2.
