Do You Need a Continuous Glucose Monitor If You're Not on Insulin?

If you have type 2 diabetes and manage it with tablets, a non-insulin injection or changes to how you eat and move, you've probably been told a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) isn't for you. For years, that was the standard advice. CGMs were for people on insulin, and everyone else got a fingerstick blood glucose meter and a blood test every few months.

That advice changed in 2026. A large trial published in October found that people with type 2 diabetes who don't use insulin brought their blood sugar down further with a CGM than without one. The American Diabetes Association (ADA) now supports CGMs for people on any diabetes treatment where one helps.

The Short Answer

  • You don't need a CGM if you're not on insulin, but there's now good evidence one can help, especially if your A1C is above your target.
  • In the CONNECT trial, people using a CGM lowered their A1C by 1.6 percentage points on average, 0.9 points more than people on routine care.
  • They also spent about 5 more hours a day with their blood sugar in the target range.
  • The trial lasted 26 weeks and didn't look at long-term complications such as heart attacks or kidney disease. Dexcom, which makes the CGM it used, paid for it.
  • Over-the-counter CGMs are sold in the US without a prescription, but they have no alarms for highs and lows, and insurance coverage for people not on insulin is still patchy.

What Is a Continuous Glucose Monitor and How Is It Different From a Finger Prick?

A continuous glucose monitor is a small sensor you wear on the back of your upper arm or on your belly. A tiny, flexible filament sits just under your skin and measures the glucose in the fluid between your cells every few minutes, day and night. The readings go to an app on your phone, so you can see your blood sugar as a line that rises and falls through the day.

A fingerstick blood glucose meter gives you one number at the moment you test. That's useful, but it misses what happens between tests, such as the rise after lunch or a high that lasts through the night. A CGM fills in those hours, which is why one week of wearing a sensor can tell you more than months of finger pricks.

Most sensors last 10 to 15 days before you replace them. Keep a fingerstick meter at home anyway, so you can double-check a reading that doesn't match how you feel.

What New Research Found About CGMs for Type 2 Diabetes Without Insulin

The CONNECT trial was the first large, randomized study of CGMs in people with type 2 diabetes who don't use insulin. Researchers at 22 primary care practices in the US enrolled 283 adults aged 21 to 84, with an average A1C of 8.8%. They published their results in The New England Journal of Medicine on October 14, 2026. Everyone kept taking their usual medicines, and around 4 in 10 were on a GLP-1 or a similar incretin-based drug.

Of the 283 adults, 145 wore a Dexcom G7 CGM for 26 weeks, while 138 carried on with routine care and a standard meter. By the end, average A1C in the CGM group had fallen from 8.8% to 7.2%, compared with 8.7% to 8.0% in the routine care group. The CGM group also spent 21 percentage points more of their day in the target range of 70 to 180 mg/dL (3.9 to 10.0 mmol/L), which the ADA puts at about 5 extra hours a day.

For the CGM group, that took the average from well above to just above the 7% A1C target many adults with diabetes aim for, and 46% got below it. The routine care group improved too, but stayed a full point above that target on average.

The better numbers didn't come with more lows. People using a CGM spent slightly less time below 70 mg/dL (3.9 mmol/L), and no one in the trial had a severe low. They were also more satisfied with how they checked their blood sugar and reported less diabetes-related distress.

What the CGM Study Doesn't Tell Us About Long-Term Diabetes Complications

The CONNECT trial measured blood sugar, and blood sugar alone. It didn't track heart attacks, strokes, kidney disease, nerve damage or eye problems, which are the complications people with diabetes most want to avoid. Lower A1C is linked to fewer of these over time, but this trial can't show that a CGM prevents them.

The trial lasted 26 weeks, so we don't yet know how long the improvement lasts. People often check a new device eagerly at first and less as the months go by. Researchers are running a 6-month extension, which will show if the gains hold at 12 months.

Dexcom funded the trial and supplied the sensors. That doesn't make the results wrong, and the study went through peer review at one of the most respected medical journals. But it's fair to know, and an independent trial with another brand of sensor would add to the evidence.

Most people in the trial also started with an A1C well above target. If your A1C is already close to 7%, you may see a smaller change than they did.

What the 2026 ADA Guidelines Say About CGMs for People Not on Insulin

The ADA updates its Standards of Care in Diabetes every year, and doctors in the US and beyond use them to guide treatment. The 2026 edition, published in January, recommends CGMs for people on insulin, for people on non-insulin medicines that can cause lows, such as sulfonylureas, and for people on any diabetes treatment where a CGM helps them manage it.

That last group is new, and it covers most people with type 2 diabetes who aren't on insulin. The guidelines also treat a CGM as an option from the time of diagnosis, instead of something you earn after other treatments stop working.

The ADA wrote this recommendation before CONNECT was published. The trial now gives it the highest grade of evidence the ADA uses, which researchers expect will push insurers and national guidelines to catch up.

Who Is Most Likely to Benefit From a CGM Without Insulin?

A CGM tends to help most when your numbers aren't where you and your doctor want them, and you're not sure why. In the CONNECT trial, everyone started with an A1C above 7%, and many were well above it. If your A1C is already at target and your routine works, a fingerstick meter may give you everything you need.

These groups are most likely to get something useful from one.

  • People whose A1C is above target even though they take their medicine. A CGM can show the meals, times of day or nights when your blood sugar runs high.
  • People who were recently diagnosed. A few weeks of data can teach you how your body responds to the foods you eat most, so you can adjust early.
  • People starting or changing a medicine, such as a GLP-1. You and your doctor can see how your numbers respond, often within days instead of waiting 3 months for the next A1C test.
  • People taking sulfonylureas, such as glipizide or gliclazide. These tablets can cause lows, which is why the ADA lists them separately in its CGM recommendation.
  • People who want to see what their habits do. Many CGM users say the most useful moment is seeing their glucose curve flatten after a short walk after dinner.

You don't have to wear one all year to benefit. Some people wear a sensor for 2 to 4 weeks after a diagnosis or a change in treatment, learn their patterns, and then go back to a fingerstick meter.

Can You Get a CGM Without a Prescription for Type 2 Diabetes?

Yes, if you live in the US. In March 2024, the FDA cleared Dexcom's Stelo, the first over-the-counter CGM, for adults who don't use insulin, including people with type 2 diabetes who take tablets. It uses the same sensor as the Dexcom G7 from the CONNECT trial, lasts up to 15 days, and sends readings to an app on your phone.

Over-the-counter CGMs don't have alarms for high or low blood sugar. That's why they're only cleared for people who don't use insulin, and why the FDA says they aren't for anyone with a history of problematic lows. If you take a sulfonylurea or have had a serious low before, a prescription CGM with alerts is the safer choice.

Abbott's Lingo is also sold over the counter, but it's formulated for people without diabetes. Abbott's Libre Rio is cleared for people with type 2 diabetes who don't use insulin, but it hasn't launched in US stores yet.

Over-the-counter sensors are paid for out of pocket. Prices change often, so check the current price on the Stelo website before you buy. Many people start with 1 or 2 sensors to see if the data helps them.

Outside the US, the rules differ by country. In the UK and Europe, ask your GP or diabetes nurse what's available to you on prescription, or which sensors you can buy yourself.

Will Insurance Pay for a CGM If You Don't Take Insulin?

Often not yet, and that's one of the biggest hurdles. Insurance rules move more slowly than medical guidelines, so many plans still follow the older rule that CGMs are for people on insulin.

Medicare covers a CGM only if you use insulin or have a history of problematic low blood sugar. Problematic lows mean either two or more readings below 54 mg/dL (3.0 mmol/L), or one severe low where you needed someone else's help. You also need a doctor's visit within 6 months before the CGM is ordered.

Private insurers and Medicaid plans set their own rules, and these vary. Some now cover CGMs for people on non-insulin medicines, often only with documented lows or prior approval.

If you'd like a CGM covered, ask your doctor to note why it would help you, such as an A1C above target or a recent change in medicine.

Then call your insurer and ask three questions. Do you cover CGMs for type 2 diabetes without insulin? Do you need prior authorization? Which brands are on your list?

How to Use a CGM Without Getting Overwhelmed by the Numbers

A CGM can show you hundreds of readings a day, and it's easy to worry over every rise. The most useful number isn't any single reading but your time in range, which is the share of the day your blood sugar sits between 70 and 180 mg/dL (3.9 and 10.0 mmol/L). For most adults with diabetes, the ADA's goal is to spend more than 70% of the day in range, which is about 17 hours.

Your blood sugar is meant to rise after you eat, so a spike after a meal is normal. What tells you more is how high it goes and how long it takes to come back down. A rise that settles within 2 to 3 hours is a very different picture from one that stays high all afternoon.

Look for patterns over a few days before you change anything. One high reading after a birthday lunch tells you very little. The same high after the same breakfast four days in a row tells you something you can act on, with your doctor or on your own.

A few habits make the first few weeks easier.

  • Check the app a few times a day at first, then settle into looking at the daily summary.
  • Write down meals, walks and medicine doses for the first 2 weeks, so you can match them to the curve.
  • Test with your fingerstick meter if a reading doesn't match how you feel.
  • Bring your CGM report to your next appointment, because it gives your doctor far more to work with than a single A1C result.

If the data starts to make you anxious instead of informed, it's fine to take a break. A CGM is a tool, and you decide how often to use it.

Your One Little Win

One little win

If your A1C is above target, ask your doctor at your next visit if a CGM could help you. Bring your last A1C result and a list of your current medicines, so you can talk through prescription and over-the-counter options together.

Frequently Asked Questions About CGMs for Type 2 Diabetes

Is a CGM Worth It If I'm Not on Insulin?

For many people, yes, especially if your A1C is above target. In the CONNECT trial, people with type 2 diabetes who didn't use insulin lowered their A1C by 0.9 percentage points more with a CGM than with routine care over 26 weeks. If your numbers are already at target, the benefit is likely to be smaller.

Can I Get a CGM Without a Prescription?

In the US, yes. Dexcom's Stelo is sold over the counter to adults who don't use insulin. It doesn't have alarms for highs or lows, so it isn't suitable if you've had serious lows before.

Will My Insurance Cover a CGM for Type 2 Diabetes?

It depends on your plan. Medicare covers CGMs only for people who use insulin or have a history of problematic lows. Some private plans cover them more widely, often with prior approval, so call your insurer to check.

Should I Get a CGM If I Have Prediabetes?

The CONNECT trial didn't include people with prediabetes, so there's no good evidence yet that a CGM helps them. If you'd like to see how meals affect you, a short trial with an over-the-counter sensor can be eye-opening, but the proven changes after a prediabetes diagnosis, such as losing a small amount of weight and moving more, will do more for you than any device.

Do People With Type 1 Diabetes Use the Same CGMs?

Yes, but they generally use prescription CGMs with alarms, because they take insulin and are at higher risk of dangerous lows. The differences between type 1 and type 2 diabetes explain why their monitoring needs aren't the same.

Does a CGM Hurt?

Most people feel a quick pinch when they apply the sensor, and then very little. The filament under your skin is thin and flexible. Some people get skin irritation from the adhesive, which a barrier patch or a different spot can help with.
