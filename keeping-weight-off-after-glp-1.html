Keeping Weight Off After a GLP-1: What Happens When You Stop or Switch

Maybe your insurance stopped covering your Wegovy, the side effects wore you down, or you've reached your goal and wonder how long you need to stay on it. Whatever the reason, the same worry tends to follow. Will the weight come back?

You're in good company with that question. About 11% of US adults were taking a GLP-1 for weight loss in 2026, up from 3% in 2024, according to Gallup figures shared in a Cleveland Clinic report on life after GLP-1 drugs. Cost, insurance limits and side effects are the most common reasons people stop.

A large Cleveland Clinic study presented at ObesityWeek on November 15, 2026 followed 20,486 adults for 2 years after they started semaglutide or tirzepatide. People who kept up some form of treatment kept off far more weight than those who stopped, and switching between semaglutide and tirzepatide gave the biggest results of any medicine option.

What the Cleveland Clinic GLP-1 Study Found About Keeping Weight Off

Cleveland Clinic researchers used health records from 20,486 adults who had taken semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy) or tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound) for at least 3 months between 2021 and 2023. They then compared what happened over 2 years along 5 different paths. The results were published in eClinicalMedicine and presented at ObesityWeek 2026.

The 5 paths were staying on the first GLP-1, switching between semaglutide and tirzepatide, moving to another obesity medicine, having bariatric surgery, or stopping treatment altogether. The other medicines included liraglutide, phentermine-topiramate, naltrexone-bupropion and orlistat.

Every group that kept up some form of treatment kept off more weight at 2 years than the group that stopped. People who switched between the two GLP-1s lost about 16% of their body weight, and those who stayed on their first medicine lost nearly 11%. That's about 26 pounds and 18 pounds for someone who started at 165 pounds (75 kg).

Moving to another obesity medicine kept off about 8%, and bariatric surgery gave the largest loss at about 28%. People who stopped with no further treatment still kept off about 4% of their starting weight on average.

In Summary

This study suggests that the weight you lose on a GLP-1 is easier to keep off when some form of treatment continues, whether that's the same medicine, a switch or another option. If you need to stop your current medicine, talk to your doctor about what comes next instead of stopping everything at once.

Switching From Semaglutide to Tirzepatide for Weight Loss

The switching results depended on which medicine people started with. People who began on semaglutide and switched to tirzepatide lost about 3 kg (6.6 lb) more over 2 years than those who stayed on semaglutide. For people who began on tirzepatide, staying on it worked better, and they lost about 4.5 kg (9.9 lb) more than those who switched to semaglutide.

In other words, the switch that helped most was moving to tirzepatide. That fits with tirzepatide working on two gut hormones, GLP-1 and GIP, while semaglutide works on GLP-1 alone.

The benefits went beyond the scale. For people with type 2 diabetes, A1C, your average blood sugar over about 3 months, improved most after bariatric surgery, followed by switching between the two GLP-1s, then staying on the original medicine. Blood pressure also came down more in the groups that kept up treatment than in the group that stopped.

If you have diabetes, using a continuous glucose monitor even when you're not on insulin can show you how your blood sugar responds after a switch.

"Obesity is a complex, chronic disease, and we cannot approach treatment with a one-size-fits-all approach," said lead author Dr. Hamlet Gasoyan. A switch is a decision to plan with your doctor, who can choose a starting dose and check that the new medicine suits you.

In Summary

If semaglutide has stopped working as well for you, this study suggests that switching to tirzepatide may help you lose more weight. If you're already on tirzepatide and doing well, staying on it looks like the better choice.

What Happens to Your Weight When You Stop a GLP-1

Most people regain some weight after stopping a GLP-1, but how much depends a lot on what happens next. An Oxford review of 37 studies in The BMJ pooled results from 9,341 people who stopped a weight-loss medicine. On average, weight came back at about 0.4 kg (0.9 lb) a month, and blood pressure and cholesterol tended to drift back too.

Weight came back faster after stopping a medicine than after finishing a diet and exercise program. Only a handful of the 37 studies looked at semaglutide or tirzepatide, though, and most followed people in controlled trials where they stopped without switching to anything else.

Real life often looks different. A Cleveland Clinic study of 7,938 adults who stopped semaglutide or tirzepatide, published in March 2026, found that people treated for obesity had lost 8.4% of their weight before stopping. A year later, they had regained only 0.5% on average, and 45% had kept their weight steady or kept losing.

A big reason was that many people didn't stop all treatment. Within a year, 27% switched to another medicine, 20% restarted their original GLP-1 and 14% kept up regular lifestyle visits with a health professional.

In Summary

Weight tends to come back when a GLP-1 stops and nothing replaces it. Many people in real life keep their weight steady by restarting, switching or getting ongoing support, so having a plan before you stop gives you the best chance of keeping your progress.

Why Weight Comes Back After Stopping a GLP-1

Weight regain after a GLP-1 is your body responding the way bodies do, not a sign that you failed. GLP-1 is a hormone your gut makes naturally, and it signals your brain and stomach that you're full. The medicines copy that signal at a much stronger level, so once you stop taking them, hunger and cravings generally return to how they were before.

Your body also pushes back against weight loss in general. After you lose weight, hunger hormones tend to rise and your body burns slightly fewer calories than before. That's why doctors treat obesity as a long-term condition, much like high blood pressure, where the benefits of a treatment last only as long as the treatment does.

Researchers think regain also happens faster when people haven't had time to build new eating and activity habits while on the medicine. That makes the months on a GLP-1 a good time to try out meals, movement and routines you can keep going afterward.

People stop for many reasons, and the most common are cost, insurance limits and side effects. Recent safety news, such as the FDA's new NAION eye warning on every GLP-1 label, has also left some people unsure about staying on their medicine. "When a patient stops a GLP-1 medication, the most important question is what comes next," said Dr. Ali Aminian, a co-author of the Cleveland Clinic study.

Ways to Keep Weight Off After a GLP-1 and Questions to Ask Your Doctor

The Cleveland Clinic study points to several paths for keeping your progress, and the right one depends on your health, your budget and how you've done on your medicine so far. Here are the options to talk through with your doctor.

  • Staying on your current medicine. People who kept taking their first GLP-1 had kept off nearly 11% of their weight at 2 years.
  • Switching. Moving from semaglutide to tirzepatide gave the most weight loss of any medicine path in the study.
  • Another obesity medicine. Options such as phentermine-topiramate or naltrexone-bupropion kept off about 8% on average, and they're worth asking about if your insurance won't cover a GLP-1.
  • Bariatric surgery. It gave the largest loss, at about 28%. Under ASMBS and IFSO surgery guidelines, it's recommended from a BMI of 35 and can be considered from 30 if other treatments haven't worked.
  • Ongoing support. In the real-world study, 14% of people kept seeing a health professional for lifestyle visits after stopping.

Habits you build now can help whichever path you choose. Research presented at the Endocrine Society's ENDO 2025 meeting found that eating more protein while on a GLP-1 may help prevent muscle loss, and experts suggest asking your doctor about adding strength training too. Regular meals and enough sleep can also make hunger easier to manage if it returns, and a 10-minute walk after dinner helps keep your blood sugar steadier after eating.

These questions can help you plan before you make any change.

  1. Can I take a lower dose to maintain my weight instead of stopping completely?
  2. Would switching to a different GLP-1 make sense for me?
  3. If cost is the problem, are there savings programs, cheaper options or other medicines my insurance covers?
  4. How quickly should I expect weight to come back if I stop, and how will we track it?
  5. Could a dietitian or a weight management program help me while I'm still on the medicine?
  6. Am I a candidate for bariatric surgery if medicines aren't enough?
  7. How will we check my blood sugar, blood pressure and cholesterol after any change?

  • Switching between semaglutide and tirzepatide gave the most weight loss of any medicine path, at about 16%, and moving to tirzepatide helped most.

Frequently Asked Questions About Keeping Weight Off After a GLP-1

Will I Regain Weight If I Stop Ozempic or Wegovy?

Many people regain some weight, especially if they stop with no other treatment. In a review of 37 studies, weight came back at about 0.4 kg (0.9 lb) a month after stopping. In a real-world Cleveland Clinic study, though, 45% of people kept their weight steady or kept losing in the year after stopping, often because they restarted, switched or got ongoing support.

Is Switching From Wegovy to Zepbound Worth It?

It may be. In the Cleveland Clinic study, people who switched from semaglutide (Wegovy) to tirzepatide (Zepbound) lost about 3 kg (6.6 lb) more over 2 years than those who stayed on semaglutide. Your doctor can tell you if a switch suits your health and your insurance.

Can I Take a Lower Dose of a GLP-1 to Maintain My Weight?

Some doctors lower the dose once you've reached your goal, but research on maintenance doses is still limited. Ask your doctor before changing your dose, and track your weight closely for a few months after any change.

How Fast Does Weight Come Back After Stopping a GLP-1?

On average, people in studies regained about 0.4 kg (0.9 lb) a month after stopping a weight-loss medicine, which is faster than after diet and exercise programs. How fast it happens for you depends on what you do next, such as switching, restarting or keeping up lifestyle support.

What Should I Do If I Can't Afford My GLP-1 Anymore?

Talk to your doctor before you stop. They may be able to suggest manufacturer savings programs, a medicine your insurance covers or another obesity medicine. In the Cleveland Clinic study, moving to another obesity medicine kept off about 8% of body weight at 2 years.
