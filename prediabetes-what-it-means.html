What to know first

Prediabetes means your blood sugar is above the typical range and below the level that would be called diabetes. An A1C of 5.7% to 6.4% is the usual marker.

It often reverses. In the largest trial on the subject, a program of modest weight loss and regular walking cut the risk of developing diabetes by 58%.

Most people meet the word prediabetes in a two-minute conversation. A nurse reads a number off a screen, a doctor says the results came back a little high, and you're back in the parking lot ten minutes later holding a printout you don't know how to read.

Prediabetes means your blood sugar is above the typical range but below the level that would be called diabetes. That's the whole definition, and it's less useful than the number sitting on your lab slip.

This piece covers what the number means, where yours falls inside the range, what the evidence says about turning it around, and the few things worth doing in the next week.

What Is Prediabetes?

Your body moves sugar out of your blood and into your cells using insulin. In prediabetes, your cells have stopped responding to insulin as well as they used to, so more sugar stays in your bloodstream than it should. That's insulin resistance, and it develops slowly over years.

Prediabetes almost never produces symptoms. That's the reason it goes unnoticed for so long, and why most people find out through a routine blood test rather than because something felt wrong. If you're checking yourself for warning signs, there usually aren't any to find.

It's also common. More than 2 in 5 American adults have prediabetes, which makes it one of the most widespread metabolic conditions in the country, and most of them don't know it. A diagnosis puts you in a very large group, and it mostly means someone finally ran the test.

What Are the Blood Sugar Levels for Prediabetes?

Three tests can identify prediabetes, and you may have had any one of them.

A1C

Measures your average blood sugar over roughly the past two to three months. Below 5.7% is typical. From 5.7% to 6.4% is prediabetes. At 6.5% or above, the result falls in the diabetes range. Our guide to reading your A1C result walks through what the test actually measures.

Fasting plasma glucose

Measures your blood sugar after at least eight hours without food. Below 100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L) is typical. From 100 to 125 mg/dL (5.6 to 6.9 mmol/L) is prediabetes. At 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) or above, the result falls in the diabetes range.

Oral glucose tolerance test

Measures your blood sugar two hours after a sugary drink. Below 140 mg/dL (7.8 mmol/L) is typical. From 140 to 199 mg/dL (7.8 to 11.0 mmol/L) is prediabetes. At 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) or above, the result falls in the diabetes range.

One thing to know before you file this away. A single high result usually isn't enough on its own. Johns Hopkins notes that doctors typically confirm a first abnormal test with a second one before recording a diagnosis, so if you've only had one test, the picture isn't complete yet.

Is an A1C of 5.7 the Same as an A1C of 6.4?

No, and this is the part that rarely gets explained.

Both numbers sit inside the prediabetes range, so both get the same label and often the same short conversation. But the distance between them is real. Research published in the BMJ found that about one in ten adults with an A1C between 6.0% and 6.4% develops diabetes each year. At the lower end of the range, the yearly risk is considerably smaller.

That's worth knowing because it changes how much time you have, not what you should do.

There's a second thing your lab slip may be telling you. If both your fasting glucose and your A1C came back elevated, that carries more weight than one alone. In a Spanish study of more than 2,000 people, metabolic syndrome was present in 76.2% of people who met both criteria, compared with around 60% of those who met only one and 15.0% of people with normal glucose readings.

So take a second look at your results before you decide how concerned to be. One test slightly over the line at 5.8% is a different situation from two tests both sitting near the top of the range, even though the word on the chart is identical.

Does Prediabetes Always Turn Into Diabetes?

No. This is the question most people are actually asking, and the honest answer is more encouraging than the diagnosis itself.

Prediabetes moves in three directions. Some people progress to type 2 diabetes. Some return to normal blood sugar. And a smaller share than you'd expect stay where they are, because research tracking people with prediabetes found that most eventually move in one direction or the other rather than staying put in the middle.

Return happens more often than most people assume. One German cohort followed adults with A1C-defined prediabetes for 12 years and found that 33.8% returned to normal blood sugar without medication.

The same body of research points to what pushes people toward diabetes. A higher body mass index, a family history of diabetes, and having both impaired fasting glucose and impaired glucose tolerance rather than fasting glucose alone all raise the odds of progression. Some of those you can't do anything about. Others you can.

Can Prediabetes Be Reversed?

Often, yes. Reversal here means your blood sugar returns to the typical range, which usually shows up as an A1C below 5.7%.

The strongest evidence comes from the Diabetes Prevention Program, a trial that ran in the United States starting in 1996 and enrolled more than 3,000 adults with prediabetes. Participants were assigned to an intensive lifestyle program, the medication metformin, or a placebo.

After an average of 2.8 years, the lifestyle group had reduced their risk of developing diabetes by 58% and the metformin group by 31% compared with placebo. The results were strong enough that the trial was stopped early.

What the lifestyle group actually did is the useful part. They aimed for 7% weight loss and at least 150 minutes of physical activity per week, and they hit both targets by the end of the first year. That was the whole intervention. Weight and movement, tracked over time with support from a coach.

The effect lasted. Researchers followed the same participants for more than two decades, and compared with the placebo group, the median time lived without diabetes was 3.5 years longer in the lifestyle group and 2.5 years longer in the metformin group.

There's also a benefit that doesn't appear on your chart. Among people in the study who returned to normal blood sugar, the prevalence of small blood vessel damage was 22% to 30% lower than in those who stayed in the prediabetes range. Those are the vessels that affect your eyes, kidneys and nerves.

A note on the word reversed

Blood sugar responds to what you're doing now, so if the changes stop, the numbers tend to drift back up. This works more like maintenance than a cure, and knowing that in advance saves a lot of frustration later.

How Long Does It Take to Reverse Prediabetes?

Give it three months before you read anything into a retest.

That's not an arbitrary waiting period. A1C reflects your average blood sugar over the past two to three months, so a test taken three weeks after you change something is mostly measuring the weeks before you changed it. People who retest early often see almost no movement and conclude that nothing's working.

From there, many people see measurable change in A1C and fasting glucose within three to six months of changing their habits. How fast it moves depends on where you started, your weight and your age, so a timeline that fits someone else won't necessarily fit you.

What to Do First if You Have Prediabetes

Three things carry most of the effect. Everything else is a refinement you can add later once these are in place.

Lose 5% to 7% of your body weight, if that applies to you. For someone at 200 pounds, that's 10 to 14 pounds. For someone at 160 pounds, it's 8 to 11. The number is smaller than most people assume when they hear the words weight loss, and the research is clear that this modest range does the work.

Get 150 minutes of movement a week. That comes out to a little over 20 minutes a day, and it counts in pieces. A walk after dinner works, and so does a longer route to the shop. Our piece on walking after meals covers why timing your movement around eating gives you a bit more return for the same effort.

Change one thing about how you eat. One. Not a new eating plan, not a list of foods you'll never touch again. Pick the change that would be easiest to still be doing in six months, because the switch you can sustain beats the overhaul that lasts nine days.

If you want somewhere to start on that last one, the changes people tend to keep are the ones that add rather than remove.

  • More protein at breakfast
  • A vegetable added to a meal you already cook
  • Water in place of one sweet drink a day
  • Fruit within reach on the counter

What to Ask Your Doctor About Prediabetes

Most people leave the appointment without asking anything, usually because nobody mentioned there was anything to ask for.

Metformin is worth raising. It reduced diabetes risk by 31% in the trial, less than the lifestyle program did, and doctors tend to consider it for people with a higher BMI, people whose numbers sit near the top of the prediabetes range, and people who've had gestational diabetes.

Prevention programs are the bigger miss. A CDC-recognized lifestyle change program can cut your risk of developing type 2 diabetes by about half, and most people have never heard these exist. They run for a year with a trained coach and a group of people in the same position.

Questions worth taking in with you:

  • When should I be retested, and can we book it now
  • Which test was I diagnosed on, and was it confirmed with a second one
  • Were my fasting glucose and A1C both elevated, or just one
  • Is metformin something you'd consider for me
  • Is there a prevention program I can be referred to

What if Your Prediabetes Numbers Go Up Anyway

Sometimes they do. People follow the plan, lose the weight, keep up the walking, and still watch their A1C climb past 6.5%.

That's not a personal failure. Insulin resistance involves genetics, age, medications you may be taking for something else, and processes nobody has full control over. And the changes you made still count. The people in the prevention trial who developed diabetes anyway had years more without it than they otherwise would have, and the work they did protected their blood vessels along the way.

One little win

Book your retest before you leave the appointment, and write today's number down with the date. A retest that exists on a calendar is a different thing from one you'll get around to, and the number gives you something to compare against in three months.

Frequently Asked Questions

What A1C level is prediabetes?

An A1C of 5.7% to 6.4% falls in the prediabetes range. Below 5.7% is typical, and 6.5% or above falls in the diabetes range. A first abnormal result is usually confirmed with a second test.

Can prediabetes go away on its own?

Blood sugar can return to a typical range without medication, and in one German cohort 33.8% of people with A1C-defined prediabetes did exactly that over 12 years. It happens far more reliably when weight and activity change rather than being left alone.

How long does it take to lower your A1C?

A1C reflects two to three months of average blood sugar, so a retest before three months tells you very little. Many people see measurable change within three to six months of changing their habits.

Is prediabetes serious?

It's a real signal and it responds well to action. Progression isn't automatic, and people who return to normal blood sugar show a lower prevalence of small blood vessel damage than those who stay in the prediabetes range.

Do you need medication for prediabetes?

Not usually. Lifestyle change outperformed metformin in the Diabetes Prevention Program, cutting risk by 58% against 31%. Metformin is still worth asking about, particularly with a higher BMI or a number near the top of the range.

What is borderline diabetes?

It's an older, informal name for prediabetes. Both describe blood sugar above the typical range and below the diabetes threshold, and the diagnostic numbers are the same.

How much weight do you need to lose with prediabetes?

Around 5% to 7% of your body weight, which is 10 to 14 pounds for someone at 200 pounds. That's the range the prevention trial targeted, and it was enough to produce a 58% reduction in risk.
