What to Ask Your Doctor at Your First Diabetes Appointment

Your first diabetes appointment is your best chance to understand your diagnosis and leave with a clear plan. Appointments tend to move fast, and with so much new information coming at once, it’s easy to forget what you meant to ask until you’re already home.

This guide covers what happens at the appointment, what to bring, and the questions to ask about:

  • your diagnosis
  • your blood sugar and A1C targets
  • medication
  • food and exercise
  • warning signs
  • your checkups and emotional health

It ends with a printable checklist you can take into the room.

What to know first

  • Write your questions down before the visit and put your top three at the start.
  • Take your medication list, any blood sugar readings, and your family health history.
  • If you can, bring someone with you to listen and take notes.
  • Ask for your care plan in writing before you leave.

What Happens at Your First Diabetes Appointment?

At your first diabetes appointment, your doctor will ask about your health history, examine you, and order blood and urine tests. Together, these show how diabetes is affecting your body now and give a starting point for your care plan.

Your doctor will probably ask about your symptoms, your diet and activity, any medicines you take, and if diabetes runs in your family. Answer as openly as you can, because your doctor uses this information to choose your treatment.

The physical exam generally includes your weight, blood pressure, and a check of your feet. Your feet are checked because diabetes can reduce feeling and blood flow there over time.

Common tests at or soon after a first visit include:

  • A1C, your average blood sugar over about three months
  • Cholesterol, because diabetes raises heart disease risk
  • Kidney tests, a blood test (eGFR) and a urine test for protein
  • Liver function, which can affect which medicines suit you

The American Diabetes Association Standards of Care recommend a referral for diabetes self-management education at diagnosis. You may be sent for an eye exam as well, since diabetes can damage the small blood vessels in the eyes before you notice any change in your vision.

A first visit often runs longer than a routine one, but time is still short. A written list helps you use that time well.

What to Bring to Your First Diabetes Appointment: A Practical Checklist

Bring a list of all your medicines and supplements, any blood sugar readings you have, your family health history, and your written questions. With these, your doctor can make safer decisions and you're more likely to get through your list.

Pack these before you go:

  • Your medicines and supplements. List prescriptions, over-the-counter pills, vitamins, and herbal products, with the dose for each. Some supplements affect blood sugar or interact with diabetes medicines.
  • Any blood sugar readings. Bring your meter or a written log with dates and times. If you don't have readings yet, that's fine.
  • Recent test results. If another clinic or lab diagnosed you, bring copies so the tests don't need repeating.
  • Your family health history. Note any relatives with diabetes, heart disease, kidney disease, or stroke.
  • Your questions, most important first. If time runs short, you'll still get answers to your top three.
  • A notebook or a trusted person. Someone with you can take notes while you listen and ask questions.
  • Your insurance card and referral letter, if your clinic needs them.

Questions to Ask About Your Diabetes Diagnosis and Type

Start by asking which type of diabetes you have and how your doctor confirmed it. Your treatment depends on the type, so get this answer first.

“Which type of diabetes do I have?”

Type 1 and type 2 are treated differently. People with type 1 need insulin from the start, while type 2 is often treated first with lifestyle changes and tablets. You can read more in what actually happens in your body when you have diabetes.

“How sure are you about the type?”

Adults are sometimes told they have type 2 when they actually have type 1 or a slower form of it. If you’re slim, younger, or losing weight without trying, ask if an antibody test would help confirm the type.

“What do my test results mean?”

Ask for your actual numbers so you can track them over time. According to the American Diabetes Association diagnosis criteria, an A1C of 6.5% (48 mmol/mol) or above points to diabetes. Our guide to what a normal A1C is explains what your number means in everyday blood sugar.

“How much has diabetes already affected my body?”

Type 2 diabetes can develop for years before anyone finds it. Your kidney, eye, and foot results show if any early damage has started and if you need extra care.

“Is this something that can improve?”

Many people with type 2 diabetes can bring their blood sugar down a lot, especially early on. Ask what change is realistic for you and how soon you might see it.

Questions to Ask About Your Blood Sugar and A1C Targets

Ask your doctor for your personal targets: your A1C goal, your blood sugar range before meals, and your limit after meals. Your targets depend on your age, your health and the medicines you take, so yours may differ from the general guide.

For many adults with diabetes, the targets listed by the National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases are:

  • A1C below 7% (53 mmol/mol)
  • 80 to 130 mg/dL (4.4 to 7.2 mmol/L) before a meal
  • below 180 mg/dL (10.0 mmol/L) one to two hours after you start eating
  • above 70 mg/dL (3.9 mmol/L) at all times, to avoid lows

“What are my targets, and why did you pick them?”

Older adults and people who have frequent lows are often given looser targets, which lowers the risk of dangerous drops. If you know the reason behind your numbers, the readings you see at home will make more sense. Our guide to normal blood sugar levels by age explains how these ranges compare with the ranges for people without diabetes.

“Do I need to check my blood sugar at home, and how often?”

People on insulin generally check several times a day. People managing type 2 with tablets or lifestyle changes may check less often, or only at certain times. Ask exactly when to check, for example before breakfast or two hours after dinner.

“Would a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) help me?”

A CGM is a small sensor worn on the arm or belly that reads your glucose every few minutes and sends the readings to your phone. It's now common for people on insulin, and more people with type 2 use one too. Ask if you qualify and what your insurance covers.

“How often will you check my A1C?”

Doctors generally repeat the A1C test every three months while treatment is being adjusted, then twice a year once your levels are stable.

Questions to Ask About Diabetes Medication and Insulin

For every medicine your doctor prescribes, ask what it does, how and when to take it, what side effects to watch for, and what to do if you miss a dose. The American Diabetes Association suggests asking these questions of your pharmacist or diabetes educator as well.

“What does this medicine do, and how will I know it's working?”

Diabetes medicines work in different ways. Some help your body use insulin better, some help your kidneys remove extra sugar, and insulin replaces what your body can't make. If you know how yours works, you'll know what change to look for in your readings.

“When and how should I take it?”

Ask if you should take it with food, at a set time of day, or around meals. Metformin, one of the most common first medicines for type 2 diabetes, can upset the stomach, and taking it with food often helps.

“What side effects should I watch for?”

Most side effects are mild and fade within a few weeks. Ask which side effects are normal and which ones mean you should call the clinic. If you take metformin for a long time, ask about checking your vitamin B12 level, since low B12 can develop over the years.

“Can this medicine make my blood sugar go too low?”

Insulin and some tablets, such as sulfonylureas, can cause low blood sugar. If yours can, ask how to spot a low and what to keep with you to treat one.

“What should I do if I miss a dose?”

The answer is different for each medicine. Some doses can be taken late, while others should be skipped until the next one is due.

“How much will this cost, and is there a cheaper option?”

Some diabetes medicines are expensive. If cost could stop you from taking a medicine regularly, tell your doctor now. They may be able to prescribe a lower-cost medicine that works in a similar way.

Questions to Ask About a Diabetic Diet and Exercise

Ask your doctor to refer you to a registered dietitian and to tell you what kind of activity is safe for you. Food and movement have a big effect on blood sugar, and personal advice works better than a general diet sheet.

“Can you refer me to a dietitian?”

No single eating plan works for everyone with diabetes. A dietitian can create a plan around the foods you already eat, your culture and your budget. In many countries, insurance covers these visits for people with diabetes.

“How much carbohydrate should I eat?”

Carbohydrates raise blood sugar more than protein or fat, so it helps to know how much to eat at each meal. If you take insulin, ask if you need to learn carb counting, since your dose may depend on how many carbs are in the meal.

“Is there any food I should avoid completely?”

Most people can still eat the foods they enjoy, in smaller portions or less often. Sugary drinks are a common exception, because they raise blood sugar fast.

“How much exercise should I aim for, and is any activity unsafe for me?”

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention recommends at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, such as brisk walking. If you take insulin or have problems with your eyes, feet or heart, ask which activities to avoid and if you should check your blood sugar before exercise. Even a short walk after meals can help bring your blood sugar down.

Questions to Ask About High and Low Blood Sugar Warning Signs

Ask your doctor what a high or low blood sugar looks like for you, what to do about each one, and when to get emergency help. Knowing the signs early lets you act before a small problem turns into a serious one.

“What are the signs of low blood sugar, and how do I treat it?”

Low blood sugar means a reading below 70 mg/dL (3.9 mmol/L). It can make you shaky, sweaty, hungry, dizzy or confused, and your heart may race. The American Diabetes Association guide to low blood sugar suggests treating a low with 15 grams of fast-acting carbohydrate, such as half a cup of juice, and checking again after 15 minutes. Ask your doctor if this rule suits you and what to keep in your bag.

“What are the signs of high blood sugar?”

High blood sugar can make you very thirsty and tired, and you may pass urine more often or have blurry vision. Ask what reading is too high for you and what to do when you see one.

“When should I call you, and when should I go to the emergency room?”

Get a clear answer and write it down. Vomiting, belly pain, fast breathing or fruity-smelling breath can be signs of diabetic ketoacidosis, a dangerous condition that needs urgent care. It's more common in type 1 diabetes, although it can happen with type 2.

“What should I do when I'm sick?”

Illness and infection can push blood sugar up even when you're eating less. Ask for sick-day rules, including how often to check your blood sugar, if any medicine should be paused, and how to keep enough fluids down.

“Do my family or coworkers need to know anything?”

If you take insulin or another medicine that can cause lows, the people around you should know the signs and what to do. Ask if you need a glucagon kit, an emergency medicine that someone else can give you during a severe low.

Questions to Ask About Your Diabetes Care Team and Regular Checkups

Ask which specialists you should see and how often you need each checkup. Diabetes care involves several health workers, and regular tests catch problems with your eyes, kidneys, feet and heart early, when they're easier to treat.

Besides your main doctor, your diabetes care team may include:

  • a diabetes educator, who teaches you daily self-care
  • a registered dietitian
  • an eye doctor
  • a foot specialist (podiatrist)
  • a dentist, since diabetes raises the risk of gum disease
  • a pharmacist
  • an endocrinologist, a doctor who specializes in diabetes and hormones

“Can you refer me to a diabetes education program?”

These programs teach you how to check your blood sugar, take your medicines, plan meals and handle lows. People often find them most useful in the first months after diagnosis.

“Do I need to see an endocrinologist?”

Many people with type 2 diabetes are cared for by their family doctor. People with type 1 diabetes, and people whose blood sugar stays high despite treatment, are often referred to an endocrinologist.

“Which checkups do I need, and how often?”

Most people with diabetes need:

  • an A1C test every three to six months
  • a blood pressure check at every visit
  • a dilated eye exam once a year, or as often as your eye doctor advises
  • a full foot exam once a year, plus a quick look at every visit
  • kidney tests and a cholesterol check at least once a year

“Which vaccines do I need?”

Diabetes can make some infections more serious. Ask which vaccines are recommended for you, such as flu, pneumonia and hepatitis B.

Questions to Ask About Diabetes Stress and Emotional Health

Tell your doctor how you're coping, and ask what support is available. Stress, worry and low mood after a diabetes diagnosis are common, and your mental health affects how well you can manage your diabetes each day.

“Is it normal to feel overwhelmed by this?”

Yes. Many people feel shock, anger, fear or guilt after a diagnosis, and the daily work of checking, dosing and planning meals can wear you down. This strain has a name, diabetes distress, and doctors can help with it. Our guide to diabetes and mental health explains the signs to watch for.

“Can you check me for depression or anxiety?”

People with diabetes are more likely to have depression and anxiety than people without it. Your doctor can screen you with a few short questions and refer you for support.

“Is there a counselor or support group you'd recommend?”

Talking with others who live with diabetes can make the early months easier. Ask about local groups, online communities run by trusted health groups, or a counselor who understands diabetes.

You don't have to work this out alone

A new diagnosis is a lot to take in, and it's normal to need time. Your doctor, diabetes educator and the people close to you are all there to help, so ask for support whenever you need it.

How to Get the Most Out of a Short Diabetes Doctor Visit

Put your three most important questions first, repeat back what you hear, and ask for your plan in writing. These habits help you remember the advice once you're home.

  • Say at the start that you have questions, so your doctor can plan the time.
  • Ask your top three questions before anything else.
  • Repeat the advice back in your own words, for example “So I take one tablet with breakfast and one with dinner.” This lets your doctor correct any mix-up straight away.
  • Ask your doctor to write down any new medicine, dose or target.
  • Ask if you can record the conversation on your phone, or let the person with you take notes.
  • Find out how to contact the clinic between visits, such as through a patient portal, by phone or through a nurse line.
  • Book your follow-up appointment before you leave.

If you run out of time, ask which questions a nurse, diabetes educator or pharmacist could answer instead.

Printable Diabetes Appointment Checklist: 15 Questions to Bring

  1. Which type of diabetes do I have, and how sure are you?
  2. What do my test results mean?
  3. Has diabetes already affected my eyes, kidneys, feet or heart?
  4. What are my A1C and blood sugar targets?
  5. How often should I check my blood sugar at home?
  6. Would a continuous glucose monitor help me?
  7. What does my medicine do, and when should I take it?
  8. What side effects should I watch for?
  9. Can my medicine cause low blood sugar?
  10. Can you refer me to a dietitian and a diabetes education program?
  11. How much exercise should I aim for, and is any activity unsafe for me?
  12. What should I do if my blood sugar is too high or too low?
  13. When should I call you, and when should I go to the emergency room?
  14. Which checkups and vaccines do I need, and how often?
  15. Who can I talk to if I’m struggling emotionally?

Frequently Asked Questions

How long is a first diabetes appointment?

A first diabetes appointment generally runs longer than a routine visit, because your doctor needs your full history and may order several tests. The time varies from clinic to clinic, so ask when you book and plan your top questions around it.

Should I bring someone to my first diabetes appointment?

Yes, if you can. A partner, relative or friend can take notes, remember details you miss and ask questions you might forget. Tell the doctor at the start that you'd like them in the room.

What tests are done at a first diabetes appointment?

Most people have an A1C test, a cholesterol check, kidney tests using blood and urine, and a blood pressure check. Your doctor may also check your liver function and examine your feet, and may refer you for an eye exam.

Do I need to fast before my first diabetes appointment?

Some blood tests, such as a fasting glucose test, need you to skip food for about 8 hours beforehand, while an A1C test doesn't. Call the clinic before your visit and ask if you should fast and if you should take your usual medicines that morning.

What if I think of more questions after the appointment?

That's very common. Write them down as they come to you, then send them through your clinic's patient portal, call the nurse line or ask your pharmacist. Keep any you can't get answered for your next visit.
